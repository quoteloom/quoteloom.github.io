How to check if a trucking load covers your cost after deadhead

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Knowing your cost per mile is only half the job. A load still has empty miles, its own tolls, and sometimes a factoring or broker percentage taken off the revenue. This page takes a cost per mile you already have and checks three example loads: two billed per loaded mile and one billed as a flat amount. The numbers are example data from the Quoteloom trucking spreadsheet, chosen so the arithmetic is visible. They are not market rates, not typical freight, and not a recommended bid. Replace every figure with your own. Planning aid only; not financial, tax, legal or business advice.

Start with a cost per mile, then size the trip

The worked examples below use the same solo owner-operator illustration as the cost-per-mile guide and the free calculator defaults: $2,200 a month truck payment, $12,000 a year insurance, $2,000 a year in permits, $40 a month for ELD, $250 a month in other fixed costs, $5.90 a gallon, 7.0 MPG, $0.20 a mile maintenance, $0.05 a mile for tires, and 100,000 miles a year including empty miles. Driver pay is $0 in this illustration, so any leftover is treated as what remains for the owner. That combination produces annual fixed costs of $43,880 and a total cost per mile of $1.5717, including an average $0.04 a mile for tolls. The other guide shows that arithmetic in full; this page does not repeat it.

For an individual trip, the spreadsheet does not simply multiply $1.5717 by trip miles. It leaves the annual average toll line out of the running cost and adds the tolls and extra costs you type for this load. The running cost per mile used below is:

Fixed $0.4388 + fuel $5.90 ÷ 7.0 + maintenance $0.20 + tires $0.05 = $1.531657… per mile, then plus this trip’s tolls and other costs. If you use the free calculator instead, it multiplies your overall cost per mile (including average tolls) by trip miles, which is why a 580-mile trip there costs $911.56 rather than the $913.36 below. Both are planning estimates. Use the same method consistently, and put this trip’s actual tolls in if you have them.

Revenue, cost, profit and a break-even loaded rate

  • Trip miles = loaded miles + deadhead miles.
  • Revenue = loaded miles × the per-mile rate, or the flat amount if the load is billed that way.
  • Cost before a revenue share = running cost per mile × trip miles + this trip’s tolls and other costs.
  • If you pay a factoring or broker fee as a percent of revenue, add that percent of revenue to the cost (or, equivalently, keep only 1 minus that percent of the revenue). The 3% used later is the factoring percentage typed in the spreadsheet’s hired-driver example. It is an illustration, not a typical factoring fee and not a quote from a factor.
  • Profit = revenue − total cost.
  • Break-even loaded rate = (cost before the revenue share) ÷ (loaded miles × the share of revenue you keep). If you keep all of the revenue, that is just cost before the share ÷ loaded miles. If loaded miles are 0, or you would keep 0% of the revenue, the rate is not reachable.

Three example loads (same truck, no factoring yet)

All three are checked with the $1.531657… running cost and 0% factoring.

Example 1 — 500-mile dry van, billed per mile. 500 loaded miles, 80 deadhead miles (580 trip miles), $2.40 per loaded mile, $25 in trip tolls.

  • Revenue = 500 × $2.40 = $1,200.00
  • Running cost = 580 × $1.531657… = $888.36, plus $25 tolls = $913.36 total cost
  • Profit = 1,200.00 − 913.36 = $286.64
  • Break-even loaded rate = 913.36 ÷ 500 = $1.8267 per loaded mile

The $2.40 rate is above that floor in this illustration. The trip’s empty share is 80 ÷ 580 ≈ 13.8%, a little below the 16% empty share assumed in the annual $1.8710 break-even loaded rate from the cost-per-mile guide, which is why this trip’s floor is a little lower.

Example 2 — short haul with more deadhead. 180 loaded miles, 90 deadhead miles (270 trip miles), $2.85 per loaded mile, $15 of other trip costs, no extra tolls.

  • Revenue = 180 × $2.85 = $513.00
  • Cost = 270 × $1.531657… + $15 = $428.55
  • Profit = $84.45
  • Break-even loaded rate = 428.55 ÷ 180 = $2.3808 per loaded mile

The line-haul rate is higher than in example 1, but a third of the miles are empty (90 ÷ 270), so the break-even loaded rate is higher too. A strong per-mile number can still be a thin load once deadhead is counted.

Example 3 — flat-rate regional. 620 loaded miles, 40 deadhead miles (660 trip miles), a $1,600.00 flat amount, $40 tolls and $75 of other trip costs.

  • Revenue = $1,600.00 (the flat amount; that is $1,600.00 ÷ 620 = $2.5806 per loaded mile, for comparison only)
  • Cost = 660 × $1.531657… + $40 + $75 = $1,125.89
  • Profit = $474.11
  • Break-even loaded rate = 1,125.89 ÷ 620 = $1.8160 per loaded mile

A flat amount hides the rate until you divide it by loaded miles and then still subtract deadhead, tolls and extra costs. These three results are not a ranking of lanes or a suggestion to prefer flat-rate work.

The same three loads after a 3% factoring fee

Now keep the same running costs and add 3% of revenue to each load’s cost. 3% is only the illustration described above.

  • Example 1: factoring $36.00, cost $949.36, profit $250.64, break-even loaded rate = 913.36 ÷ (500 × 0.97) = $1.8832
  • Example 2: factoring $15.39, cost $443.94, profit $69.06, break-even loaded rate = $2.4545
  • Example 3: factoring $48.00, cost $1,173.89, profit $426.11, break-even loaded rate = $1.8721

The fee comes off the top of revenue, so the loaded rate you need rises. Replace 3% with the percentage that actually applies to you, or use 0% if you are not factoring that load.

Questions

Why is the break-even rate on example 1 $1.8267 here and $1.8231 in the cost-per-mile guide?

They use slightly different toll treatments. That guide’s free-calculator method multiplies the $1.5717 overall cost per mile (which already includes $0.04 a mile of average tolls) by 580 miles, for a $911.56 cost and a $1.8231 break-even loaded rate. This page uses the spreadsheet’s trip method: a $1.531657… running cost plus the $25 typed for this load, for a $913.36 cost and $1.8267. Neither figure is a recommended bid.

Does a profitable example mean I should take that load?

No. These are planning illustrations on sample numbers. They leave out income taxes, detention, fuel-tax credits, and a wait that runs longer than you planned. Verify every number and consult qualified professionals before you bid or dispatch.

Is 3% a typical factoring fee?

This page does not say so. 3% is the percentage typed in the spreadsheet’s hired-driver example, used here only so you can see the arithmetic. Use the rate in your own agreement, or 0% if you do not factor.

Is this business or freight-rate advice?

No. It is a planning layout with worked examples. It is not a market rate, not a forecast, and not tax, legal or business advice.
