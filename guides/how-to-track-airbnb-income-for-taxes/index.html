How to track short-term rental income for taxes: a record-keeping layout, not tax advice

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

This page is about the records you keep during the year: each payout, each channel fee, each turnover cost and each other expense, totaled by property and by month. It is not tax advice. It does not tell you what is income, what is deductible, which form to file, or how to treat a property under tax law. Those questions are for a qualified tax professional and for the rules that apply to you.

The worked figures are the same fictional April example used in the owner-statement guide and in Rental Host OS. They are independently recomputed here. They are illustrations, not a forecast and not a sample return.

What to write down for each stay

One row per booking is enough if it includes:

  • The property, the dates, and a booking number or guest name you can trace later.
  • The stay amount, the guest cleaning fee, and the booking total.
  • The channel fee in money, not just a percentage, and the payout that actually arrived.
  • The turnover cost you paid for that stay, which may differ from the guest cleaning fee.

In the Oak Lane Cottage example, three April stays paid out as follows (a 3% channel fee is an illustration, not a published platform rate):

  • B-1001: booking total $680.00, channel fee $20.40, payout $659.60.
  • B-1002: booking total $865.00, channel fee $25.95, payout $839.05.
  • B-1003: booking total $495.00, channel fee $14.85, payout $480.15.

April payouts on this unit: 659.60 + 839.05 + 480.15 = $1,978.80. The three channel fees total $61.20. Keep both the payout and the fee. Do not treat the guest’s booking total as if it were cash you received.

What to write down besides the stays

In the same April example, three turnovers were paid at $80.00 each, so cleaning costs are $240.00. Other expenses on this unit are supplies $35.00 and internet $60.00, together $95.00. One paid repair is $18.00. Those are costs you recorded; this page does not say whether any of them is deductible.

If you also record business miles, write the date, the purpose, and the miles. The IRS published a standard mileage rate of 76 cents per mile for self-employed and business use from July 1 to December 31, 2026. Citing that rate is not tax advice, and this page does not tell you to use it on a return. It is only a published figure you might use as a travel-cost illustration, the same way the turnover guide does.

A monthly owner statement is one way to total the month: income (payouts) minus cleaning costs, other expenses and maintenance. In this example, 1,978.80 − 240.00 − 95.00 − 18.00 = $1,625.80 net for Oak Lane in April. That net is a planning total, not a taxable-income figure.

Questions

Is this tax advice?

No. This page does not tell you what to report, what you may deduct, which form to use, or how a short-term rental is treated under tax law. A qualified tax professional can use these kinds of records; the records are not a return.

Should I save the booking total or the payout?

Save both, and the channel fee that sits between them. The payout is what arrived. The booking total is what the guest was charged. In the example, the April payouts are $1,978.80 and the channel fees are $61.20.

Do I have to use a Notion template?

No. A spreadsheet is enough if every booking and every cost has a date, a property and an amount you can still explain months later.

Can I use the IRS mileage rate on this page as a deduction?

This page does not say that. It only cites the published 76-cent business rate for July–December 2026 as a figure you might use when you record travel cost. Whether and how mileage belongs on a return is a tax question, not a planning-layout question.
