How to compare a finished service job with the quote you sent

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

A cost check before you send a quote tells you whether the number is worth offering. A second check after the job tells you whether that estimate was right. The customer’s invoice does not have to change just because the work ran long. The review is for you: so the next similar job is quoted from better information.

This page uses the same pressure-washing illustration as the quote-writing guide and the pressure-washing pricing guide: a $866.40 quote and a planned direct cost of $311.30. It then adds a fictional “actuals” column. Those actuals are an illustration, not a report of a real job and not a market rate. The page is a planning aid, not pricing, tax or legal advice.

What to compare

Write down three columns for the same job: quoted price (what you sent, and invoiced if the customer accepted it), planned cost (the cost check you ran before you sent it), and actual cost (labor, materials and travel you recorded when the work was done).

Actual profit = quoted price − actual cost

Cost variance = actual cost − planned cost (a positive number means the job cost more than you planned)

Use the same cost categories on both sides so the variance is meaningful: labor you paid (or paid yourself), chemicals or supplies, and travel. The free calculators, and this example, do not allocate equipment wear or monthly overhead. If you ignore those, both the planned margin and the actual margin look fatter than the margin you keep.

Do not quietly add a new fee to the invoice after the work unless you and the customer already agreed a change. If the scope grew, say so and agree the change before you do the extra work.

Worked example: the $866.40 pressure-washing job

As in the quote-writing guide, the example quote is a house wash $480.00 + driveway $162.00 + gutters $270.00 = $912.00, minus a 5% discount of $45.60, for a total of $866.40.

The planned cost check on that job is labor $232.10 + chemicals $62.40 + travel $16.80 = $311.30. Planned profit at $866.40 is $555.10.

Now suppose the work ran long and used more chemical than that plan. In this illustration, actual labor is $277.10 ($45.00 higher), actual chemicals are $78.40 ($16.00 higher), and travel is unchanged at $16.80.

Actual direct cost: 277.10 + 78.40 + 16.80 = $372.30.

Actual profit, if you still invoice the agreed $866.40: 866.40 − 372.30 = $494.10.

Cost variance: 372.30 − 311.30 = $61.00 over the plan. Profit is $61.00 lower than the $555.10 you saw on the cost check ($45.00 + $16.00). The job is still profitable in this illustration. It is less profitable than the quote-time check said, which is the point of writing the actuals down.

You can run the same three-column review on a house-cleaning visit. The quote-writing guide’s cleaning example is a $174.50 biweekly clean with a fridge add-on and a planned cost of $103.90. If actual labor and supplies came in higher, subtract them from $174.50 the same way. Use the pressure washing calculator or the house cleaning calculator for the planned column, then replace those cost lines with what you recorded.

Questions

Should I change the invoice if the job took longer than I planned?

Not automatically. If the customer accepted a quoted price for a defined scope, that is usually what you invoice. This review is so you can quote the next similar job more carefully. If the scope grew, agree the change before you do the extra work.

Which costs belong in the comparison?

Use the same categories on the plan and on the actuals: labor, materials or chemicals, and travel. This example does not include equipment wear or monthly overhead. If you add those, add them to both columns.

What if actual profit is much lower than the cost check?

Treat it as information. Look at whether the time, the materials or the travel were wrong in the estimate, and whether the scope was clear. Do not invent a fee after the fact, and do not invent a review or a discount to make up the difference.

Is this pricing advice?

No. It is a planning layout with a worked example that reuses numbers already shown in the quote-writing and pressure-washing guides. It is not a market rate and not a guarantee of profit.
