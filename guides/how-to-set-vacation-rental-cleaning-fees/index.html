How to set a vacation rental cleaning fee, with a worked example

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

The cleaning fee a guest pays is not automatically equal to what a turnover costs you. This page shows how to build a fee from your own labor, supplies and travel, then how a percentage fee on the booking can shrink what you keep on that line. It is a planning aid, not a market survey, not a staffing plan, and not pricing, tax or legal advice.

The worked example reuses the same illustration as the turnover checklist guide: a 3-hour turnover with a $25.00 labor rate, $10.00 of supplies, 12 miles at the IRS published 76 cents business mileage rate for July–December 2026 (used here only as a travel-cost figure, not as tax advice), and a $125.00 guest fee with a 3% channel fee. Those numbers are illustrations, not typical rates.

Start from the cost, then choose a leftover

Write down what one turnover actually costs you on this unit:

Turnover cost = labor you pay + supplies + travel (and laundry, if you pay it separately)

In this example: labor 3.0 × 25.00 = $75.00, supplies $10.00, travel 12 × 0.76 = $9.12. Turnover cost = $94.12.

Next, decide the leftover you want on the cleaning line before any channel fee — not as a rule, just as a number you are willing to live with. If that leftover is $30.88, a fee with no channel percentage is:

Fee = turnover cost + leftover = 94.12 + 30.88 = $125.00.

If a booking site charges a percentage on the whole booking, that percentage usually applies to the cleaning fee too. Then:

Net cleaning revenue = fee − (fee × channel fee percent)

Leftover after the channel fee = net cleaning revenue − turnover cost

To hit a leftover after the channel fee, work backwards:

Fee = (turnover cost + leftover you want after the fee) ÷ (1 − channel fee percent)

In this example, 94.12 + 27.13 = $121.25, and 121.25 ÷ 0.97 = $125.00. A $125.00 fee, a 3% channel fee of $3.75, and net cleaning revenue of $121.25 leave $27.13 after the $94.12 cost. 3% is an illustration, not a platform’s published fee. Replace it with the rate that actually applies to you.

If you instead paid a flat $80.00 to a cleaner and had the same $10.00 of supplies and $9.12 of travel, turnover cost would be $99.12, and the leftover after a 3% fee on a $125.00 guest fee would be 121.25 − 99.12 = $22.13. A flat cleaner fee is only a good deal if it actually covers the time and travel on this unit.

A leftover on the cleaning line is not the profit of the stay. Housing costs, utilities and empty nights still have to be covered by the stay itself. Use the short-term rental profit calculator for that fuller picture. A free printable turnover checklist is also on this site.

Record the fee you charged and the cost you paid

Once you have a fee, write down both numbers on each stay: what the guest paid for cleaning, and what that turnover cost you. Those two amounts belong on a monthly owner statement — the fee is already inside the payout, and the cost is subtracted as cleaning cost. Questions

Is $125.00 a typical vacation rental cleaning fee?

This page does not say so. $125.00 is the illustration already used in the turnover guide, chosen so the arithmetic is visible. Replace it with a fee that covers your own cost and the leftover you are willing to accept.

Does a booking site’s percentage fee apply to the cleaning fee?

Often yes, when the fee is part of the booking total, but you should check the rules for your channel. In the example, 3% of $125.00 is $3.75, which is why the leftover after the fee is smaller than $30.88.

Should the cleaner’s pay equal the guest fee?

Not automatically. They are different decisions. Pay what the work actually costs; charge a fee that covers that cost and any leftover you need after channel fees. The two numbers can match, but they do not have to.

Is this pricing advice?

No. It is a planning layout with a worked example. It is not a market rate, not a forecast, and not tax, legal or investment advice.
