How to quote driveway and concrete cleaning

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Driveways look like the easiest job in pressure washing: flat, open, no ladders. That is exactly why they get underbid. The square footage is easy to guess wrong, oil spots eat time nobody priced, a one-car pad costs nearly as much to set up as a three-car drive, and the wash water has to go somewhere legal.

This guide builds a driveway and flatwork price from the work: measuring, choosing per square foot or per job, the equipment that sets your speed, stain add-ons, sealing as a separate upsell, a minimum, travel, and a worked example with a cost check. Example rates show the math; they are not a price list for your area.

What 2026 cost guides say homeowners pay

Angi’s 2026 driveway pressure washing cost guide (updated October 16, 2026) puts professional driveway pressure washing at $100–$350, with a national average of $210. It lists $0.30–$0.55 per square foot, or $60–$125 per hour for pros who bill by the hour, and says most jobs wrap up in one to two hours. By size, it shows $60–$110 for 200 sq ft, $175–$320 for 576 sq ft (which it calls the most common size), $240–$440 for 800 sq ft and $360–$660 for 1,200 sq ft.

Angi also prices by material: concrete $120–$190, asphalt $125–$195, brick $130–$200, and pavers and gravel $135–$210. It notes that asphalt needs a lower pressure setting, pavers have joints the water has to work through, and gravel can be displaced. It says smaller jobs such as a one-car driveway may trigger a minimum charge.

These are national figures, not a quote for a specific driveway. Use them to spot a rate that is far off, not as your rate card.

Step 1: measure every surface you will clean

Price the area you actually wash, not the driveway “size” the customer remembers. Break the job into simple shapes and add them up:

  • Rectangles: length × width. A 24 ft × 36 ft drive is 864 sq ft.
  • Triangles and flares: ½ × base × height. A flared apron at the street is often a triangle on each side.
  • Curves: for a quarter-circle turn, 0.785 × radius². Round up rather than chase precision.
  • Walkways, porch, side slab, public sidewalk: list each one as its own line, so the customer can see what is included and drop items if needed.

A measuring wheel on site is the most reliable method. If you estimate remotely from a map, confirm on arrival before you start, and put the measured square footage on the quote so a disagreement is about numbers, not memory. Note the surface type too: concrete, asphalt, pavers and gravel are cleaned differently.

Step 2: per square foot or per job?

Per square foot

The cleanest method, and the one Angi says is the norm. Build your base rate from your own production: rate per sq ft = (labor cost per crew-hour ÷ sq ft per crew-hour + chemical cost per sq ft) ÷ (1 − target margin). Time a few real driveways from unloading to driving away before you trust a production figure.

Per job (flat tiers)

Homeowners like a simple number: “one-car,” “two-car,” “three-car or long drive.” That is fine, as long as each tier is per-square-foot math rounded to a clean price, with a stated size limit (for example, “two-car, up to 700 sq ft”). Anything bigger, or with walkways and patios, goes back to per-square-foot pricing.

Hourly

Useful as an internal check and for odd jobs such as a heavily stained shop floor. Customers usually prefer a firm price, so estimate the hours and quote a total.

Step 3: surface cleaner vs. wand (it sets your speed)

A surface cleaner (a spinning bar of nozzles under a round hood) cleans open, flat concrete more evenly than a wand and is usually faster, and it helps avoid the streaky “zebra stripe” look that wand passes can leave on a slab. A wand is still needed for edges, steps, expansion joints, around parked cars and along walls. Most driveway jobs use both: surface cleaner for the field, wand for the edges, then a rinse.

For pricing, this matters in two ways. First, your sq ft per hour depends on the tool and the machine’s flow, so measure your own production with your own equipment rather than borrowing someone else’s. Second, jobs with lots of edges, steps, or narrow paths are slower per square foot than one open slab; add a surcharge or price those areas at a higher rate. Hot water can help on oily surfaces. Cement Concrete & Aggregates Australia’s data sheet on removing stains from concrete notes that some pressure washers allow higher-temperature water to improve how cleaning chemicals perform. If you own a hot-water unit, its fuel and wear belong in your cost.

Not every surface takes the same treatment. Angi notes asphalt should be washed on a lower setting and gravel can be displaced, so price those as separate services, not as “concrete.”

Step 4: price oil and rust stains as add-ons

A general wash removes dirt, mildew and grime. Set-in oil and rust are separate work with their own chemicals and dwell time, so quote them as separate lines, per spot or per time block.

  • Oil and grease: the CCAA data sheet says these can be hard to remove completely because they penetrate concrete quickly. Its method is to soak up surface oil with an absorbent, apply an engine degreaser or other specific product and rinse thoroughly, or use a poultice left under plastic for 24 hours, repeated if deeply ingrained oil keeps rising.
  • Rust: the same data sheet says to brush off excess rust, then apply a proprietary rust-stain product, which typically contains oxalic or phosphoric acid, following the maker’s safety instructions, rinse it off thoroughly, and test it first on an inconspicuous area. Deep rust may need a poultice.

Two pricing consequences. First, sell improvement, not guaranteed removal: write “treat and lighten oil stains” on the quote, not “remove oil stains.” Second, CCAA warns that cleaned areas may differ in color and texture from the surrounding concrete, so the whole area may need treatment for a consistent look. Mention that before you start, and offer it as an option.

Step 5: keep the wash water legal (and price it)

Driveway wash water carries oil, dirt and whatever you sprayed. Local rules vary, so check your city or county stormwater program. As one example, Gwinnett County, Georgia’s surface cleaning guideline requires reasonable dry cleanup first (such as absorbents on oil and sweeping loose dirt), says washwater must never enter a storm drain except under narrow conditions it sets out, and says leaving washwater on pavement to evaporate is not acceptable disposal in general. CCAA’s data sheet likewise says to plan for removing or disposing of wash water and contaminants before stain removal starts.

Where you have to block drains, use absorbents or recover water, that is paid time and equipment. Put it in your setup time or as a line item; do not absorb it for free.

Step 6: offer sealing as a separate upsell

Sealing is a different service with its own materials, drying time and usually a second visit, so quote it separately rather than folding it into the wash. Concrete Network’s concrete driveway cost guide (updated January 13, 2026) says professional sealing averages $1–$2 per square foot, and DIY materials run about $0.50–$0.75 per square foot. Angi lists driveway sealing as an add-on at $280–$860.

The selling point is real: CCAA says a suitable sealer can reduce how far contaminants penetrate and make later stain removal easier. Follow the sealer maker’s instructions on surface dryness, temperature and recoat timing, and price your material from your own coverage per gallon. If you do not seal yet, you can still quote “clean now, seal-ready” and refer the sealing job.

Step 7: set a minimum and charge travel

Every job has the same fixed block: driving, unloading, hooking up water, dry cleanup, blocking drains, rinsing and packing up. On a small slab, that block is most of the job. Set a minimum from your own cost to show up and apply it as job price = the greater of (sum of line items) and your minimum.

For travel, use your real cost per mile on the round trip, not the one-way distance. Many operators keep the minimum for nearby jobs and add a travel line beyond a set radius; either way, write the number down so you charge it the same way every time.

Worked example: a two-car driveway with walkways and stains

A house has a 24 ft × 36 ft concrete driveway, a 4 ft × 30 ft front walkway and 50 ft of 4 ft public sidewalk. There are two oil spots where cars park and one rust stain from a planter. One technician, surface cleaner plus wand. These are example rates and costs to show the method, not recommended prices:

The example base rate sits at the bottom of Angi’s $0.30–$0.55 band, and the total with stain work lands inside Angi’s $360–$660 band for 1,200 sq ft.

Now check the price against cost. Example production: 800 sq ft per hour with a surface cleaner, including edging. Add 0.75 hours to set up, sweep, put absorbent on the oil and block the drain, 0.5 hours for stain treatment and dwell, and 0.25 hours to rinse and pack up. That is 1,184 ÷ 800 + 0.75 + 0.5 + 0.25 = 2.98 worker-hours.

Labor cost should be what a worker-hour really costs you, not just the wage. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 reports wages and salaries were 70.0% of private-industry compensation costs. Using that national average as a rough guide, an example $20 wage becomes $20 ÷ 0.70 = $28.57 per worker-hour. Yours may differ.

  • Labor = 2.98 × $28.57 = $85.14
  • General cleaning chemicals (example $0.02 per sq ft, from your own tracking) = 1,184 × $0.02 = $23.68
  • Degreaser and rust remover (example) = $12.00
  • Absorbent and consumables (example) = $5.00
  • Equipment wear allowance (example per job) = $15.00
  • Travel = 30 round-trip miles × $0.70 (example) = $21.00

Job cost = $161.82. Profit = $440.20 − $161.82 = $278.38, a 63.2% margin before overhead. The minimum price for a 35% target margin = $161.82 ÷ 0.65 = $248.95. Insurance, the truck, marketing and slow days still come out of that profit.

Sealing upsell (example): sealing just the 864 sq ft driveway at an example $1.25 per sq ft is $1,080, within Concrete Network’s $1–$2 range. Quote it as a separate visit after the slab is dry, with your own material cost and time.

Now a small job: a 10 ft × 20 ft one-car pad is 200 sq ft. At $0.30 that is $60, which matches the bottom of Angi’s 200 sq ft band. But the time is 200 ÷ 800 + 0.75 + 0.25 = 1.25 worker-hours ($35.71), plus $4 of chemicals, $5 of consumables, $15 of wear and $21 of travel: a cost of $80.71. At $60 you lose $20.71. At the example $175 minimum the margin is 53.9%. If a customer balks at the minimum, offer to add the walkway, patio or trash-can pad for the same visit instead of cutting the price.

Common underbidding mistakes

  1. Guessing the area. Measure, list every surface and write the square footage on the quote.
  2. Free stain work. Oil and rust need their own chemicals and dwell time. Price them per spot.
  3. Promising stain removal. Promise treatment and improvement; deep oil may not come out completely.
  4. One rate for every surface. Asphalt, pavers and gravel are not open concrete.
  5. No minimum. Small slabs carry nearly the same setup and cleanup time as large ones.
  6. Ignoring wash water. Drain blocking and recovery are paid time where your local rules require them.
  7. Bundling sealing into the wash. It is a separate service with its own materials and drying time.
  8. Using wage instead of full labor cost. Payroll costs and drive time belong in the cost.
  9. Forgetting the round trip. Charge travel on the real round-trip miles.

Check the margin before you send the number.
