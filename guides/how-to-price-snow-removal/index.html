How to price residential snow removal per push vs a seasonal contract

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Snow work is sold two ways. A per-push or per-visit price is paid each time you clear the driveway. A seasonal contract is one price for the winter, often with a trigger depth and a stated number of visits, or with “unlimited” visits that shift the weather risk to you. This page shows how to compare the two, then checks an illustration against labor, equipment time, travel and a target margin.

The free lawn care calculator does not have a snow-removal line. Its default services are mowing, edging, trimming, leaf cleanup, fertilizing and aeration. The cost check below still uses that calculator’s example defaults for wage, crew size, equipment time, setup, mileage and a 30% target margin, because the arithmetic is the same: visits × your price, minus labor, equipment and travel. Published 2026 ranges, and what they are not

Angi’s snow-removal cost guide, updated 15 October 2026, reports a national average of $126 per visit from 5,994 verified projects, with most homeowners paying between $51 and $203. In the same article, Angi lists these 2026 ranges, which are what homeowners are quoted, not recommended bids:

  • Per visit, driveway and front walkway: $40–$100
  • Per hour: $25–$75
  • Residential seasonal contract: $300–$700
  • By driveway size, per visit and seasonal: single-car $40–$75 a visit and $300–$400 a season; two-car $75–$125 a visit and $400–$600 a season; four-car $125–$250 a visit and $600–$1,000 a season

Angi also lists commercial lots separately ($200–$400 a visit, $1,000–$2,000 a season, and commercial contracts $500–$2,000). This page is about a residential driveway. Salt, sidewalks, after-hours calls and roof work are extras Angi mentions; price them as their own lines. Your town’s snowfall will not match a national average.

How many visits make a seasonal contract the cheaper option for the customer

Divide the seasonal price by the per-visit price. That is how many visits the contract “covers” before the customer would have paid more per push. Using only Angi two-car endpoints:

  • $400 ÷ $125 = 3.2 visits; $400 ÷ $75 = 5.33 visits
  • $600 ÷ $125 = 4.8 visits; $600 ÷ $75 = 8.00 visits

If you expect fewer visits than that range, a per-push price can be simpler and less risky for you. If you expect more, a seasonal contract may be the number the customer wants, and you need a cost check at the high end of the visit count, not only at the average. These divisions are arithmetic on Angi ranges, not a forecast of how often it will snow.

Worked example: one two-car driveway, per visit and by the season

Illustration, not a measured job: a two-car driveway, 0.50 hours of on-site work per visit, plus the calculator’s example 10 minutes of setup, a two-person crew at $20.00 per person-hour, equipment at $9.00 per hour, an 8-mile trip at $0.75 per mile (the calculator multiplies miles × rate once per visit), and a 30% target margin. Overhead is left at $0, as on the live calculator. The 0.50-hour on-site time is a sample input so the formulas are visible, not a production rate you should copy.

One visit

  • Hours in the calculator’s model: 0.50 + (10 ÷ 60) = 0.667 hours
  • Labor: 0.667 × 2 × $20.00 = $26.67
  • Equipment: 0.667 × $9.00 = $6.00
  • Travel: 8 × $0.75 = $6.00
  • Total cost = $38.67. Minimum price for a 30% margin = 38.67 ÷ 0.70 = $55.24

If you charged $75 for that visit, which is the low end of Angi two-car per-visit range and is used here only as a comparison point, not as a recommended bid: profit = 75.00 − 38.67 = $36.33, margin 48.4%. That margin still ignores salt, a second pass, sidewalks and overhead.

A $500 seasonal contract (an illustration inside Angi two-car seasonal range of $400–$600):

  • At 8 visits: hours = 8 × 0.667 = 5.333 hours. Labor $213.33, equipment $48.00, travel $48.00. Cost = $309.33. Profit = 500.00 − 309.33 = $190.67. Margin = 38.13%. Minimum for a 30% margin = 309.33 ÷ 0.70 = $441.90. Status: meets the example target on these inputs.
  • At 12 visits: hours = 12 × 0.667 = 8.00 hours. Labor $320.00, equipment $72.00, travel $72.00. Cost = $464.00. Profit = 500.00 − 464.00 = $36.00. Margin = 7.20%. Minimum for a 30% margin = 464.00 ÷ 0.70 = $662.86. Status: below the example target. The same $500 contract that cleared 30% at 8 visits does not clear it at 12.

That is the weather risk in the seasonal number. If you sell “unlimited” visits, run the cost check at a high visit count for your town, not only at a quiet winter. Salt, sidewalks and a trigger-depth callback are separate lines. These results are not typical margins.

Questions

Does the lawn care calculator include snow removal?

No. Its default lines are mowing, edging, trimming, leaf cleanup, fertilizing and aeration. This page reuses its labor, equipment, travel and margin formulas only. You have to enter your own snow price, visit count and time.

How many storms make a seasonal contract a better deal?

Divide the contract by your per-visit price. On Angi two-car endpoints that is about 3.2 to 8 visits, depending on which ends of the ranges you use. That is a comparison, not a forecast of snowfall.

Are $75 to $125 typical prices for a two-car driveway?

This page does not say so. Those figures are Angi 2026 homeowner ranges, updated 15 October 2026, not a recommended bid and not a guarantee of what your market will pay. The $500, 8-visit and 12-visit figures are illustrations.

Is this pricing advice?

No. It is a planning layout with a worked example. It is not a market rate, not a weather forecast, and not tax, legal, insurance, safety or business advice. Plowing and de-icing have equipment, property-damage and contract terms this page does not cover. Verify every number before you send a quote.
