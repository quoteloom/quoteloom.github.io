Rental arbitrage: can re-listing a leased unit cover the rent?

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Not legal, financial, investment or tax advice. “Rental arbitrage” means leasing a home or apartment from a landlord and re-listing it as a short-term or furnished rental, hoping guest income covers the rent and everything else. You own nothing, so the whole plan rests on two questions: are you allowed to do it, and does the math still work in a slow month? This guide covers both, with a worked example you can redo in the free Quoteloom Airbnb profit calculator.

First: are you allowed to sublet or host at all?

Many leases forbid subletting or short-term guests, many HOAs and co-op boards restrict rentals, and many cities limit, license or ban short-term rentals. Check permission before you spend anything on furniture. Airbnb’s own responsible hosting page tells hosts to check HOA or co-op board regulations for any prohibition against subletting or other restriction against hosting, to read the lease and check with the landlord, and to look up permits, registrations, zoning, taxes and business license requirements that may apply. It also notes that special rules may apply in rent-controlled, rent-stabilized, public or subsidized housing.

  • Lease: get written permission for short-term guests from the landlord, ideally as a lease clause or rider that names the use, who pays for damage and any revenue share. A verbal “sure” is not enough.
  • Building or HOA: ask for the current rules in writing. A landlord cannot always override an HOA.
  • City and county: check whether a permit, registration or primary-residence rule applies, and who collects lodging tax.
  • Insurance: Airbnb’s page says its host protections do not take the place of homeowner’s or renter’s insurance or adequate liability coverage. Ask an agent about a policy that covers paying guests.

If any of these answers is “no” or “unclear,” the deal does not work, whatever the spreadsheet says. Hosting without permission can end in eviction, fines or a removed listing.

The money test: what has to be covered

Use one year of 365 nights and one average rate and occupancy (a simplification; real listings are seasonal):

  • Occupied nights = 365 × occupancy. Stays = occupied nights ÷ average length of stay.
  • Gross booking = occupied nights × nightly rate + stays × guest cleaning fee.
  • Platform fee: as of October 13, 2026, Airbnb’s host fee article states a single 15.5% service fee paid by hosts, applied to the nightly price and optional fees such as cleaning, deducted from the payout (16% for listings in Brazil and Mexico). Other channels charge differently.
  • Lodging tax you remit (local; look up your rate and whether the platform collects it).
  • Per-stay costs: your cleaning cost, laundry and supplies.
  • Monthly fixed costs: rent, utilities, internet, software, insurance and a reserve for restocking and small repairs. Unlike an owner, you pay rent every month whether the calendar is full or empty.
  • Upfront cash: furniture and setup, security deposit, first month’s rent, permits. It only pays back if the lease lasts long enough.

From that: annual cash flow = net booking − per-stay costs − 12 × monthly fixed costs, and break-even occupancy = annual fixed costs ÷ contribution at 100% occupancy.

It covers the rent, barely. Now the questions that decide the deal:

  • Break-even occupancy. At 100% occupancy the contribution would be $39,538.62, so break-even = $23,160 ÷ $39,538.62 = 58.6%. The example’s 60% leaves a margin of about 1.4 points.
  • Maximum rent you can afford. At 60% occupancy, the contribution minus utilities and other fixed costs is $23,723.18 − 12 × $330 = $19,763.18, or $1,646.93 a month. Any rent above that loses money even before setup costs.
  • Stress tests. At 50% occupancy cash flow is −$3,390.69 a year (−$282.56 a month); at 45% it is −$5,367.62. A 10% lower nightly rate at 60% occupancy gives −$1,843.63.
  • Setup payback. Cash in = $9,000 + $1,600 + $1,600 = $12,200. At $46.93 a month that would take about 260 months to recover, far longer than a 12-month lease. Spread the $9,000 of furniture over a 12-month lease ($750 a month) and the example loses about $703 a month.

The lesson is not that rental arbitrage never works. It is that a deal which “covers the rent” on average can still fail on setup cost, a slow season or a short lease. You want a clear cushion above break-even and a lease long enough to recover the setup.

A quick checklist before you sign

  1. Written landlord permission, HOA/building rules and city rules checked (see above).
  2. Rate and occupancy from your own research on comparable listings for each month, not a single optimistic average.
  3. Break-even occupancy comfortably below the occupancy you expect in your slow months.
  4. Rent at or below the maximum affordable rent at a conservative occupancy.
  5. Setup cash recovered well within the lease term, with a plan if the lease is not renewed.
  6. Insurance that covers paying guests, and a reserve for damage, vacancies and repairs.

Common mistakes

  1. Skipping permission. No lease clause, HOA approval or permit means no deal.
  2. Using peak-season numbers for the whole year. Rent is due every month.
  3. Forgetting the host fee on the cleaning fee. Airbnb’s 15.5% applies to optional fees too.
  4. Ignoring setup payback. Furniture, deposit and first month’s rent are real cash.
  5. Treating cash flow as profit after tax. Income tax is not in this model; ask a tax professional.
