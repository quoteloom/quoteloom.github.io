How to price the same window-cleaning job per pane and per window

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

A “window” and a “pane” are not the same unit of work. One opening can be a single sheet of glass, or it can hold six, eight or more small panes in a grid or French sash. If you count openings and apply a rate meant for a standard window, divided-light work is easy to underprice. If you count panes and apply a small-pane rate to a large picture window, you can underprice that glass instead. This page prices one example house both ways, then checks each quote against labor, supplies, travel, equipment and a share of overhead.

The rates, production times and cost assumptions are the example defaults shipped in the Quoteloom window and gutter spreadsheet, used here so the arithmetic can be checked. They are not market rates, not typical production, and not a recommended bid. The window-cleaning bidding guide covers when to use per window, per pane, per hour or per square foot, plus add-ons and minimums. This page does not repeat that survey. The example house

A one-story house with 12 openings, all on the first floor. Each opening is a divided-light (French or grid) window with 8 panes, so there are 96 panes of glass. The customer wants inside and outside, plus 12 screens and 12 tracks and sills. Travel in this illustration is 12 miles, billed at the spreadsheet’s example $0.70 per mile. The visit is a one-time cleaning, so no recurring discount applies. The house, pane count and rates are sample data, not a typical job.

Example unit rates used below: per window, $6.00 exterior and $5.00 interior; per pane, $1.50 exterior and $1.25 interior; screens $3.00 each; tracks and sills $3.00 per opening. The spreadsheet also applies a $150 window-work minimum if a small job would otherwise come in below that; both quotes here are above $150, so the minimum does not change the price.

Priced per window (12 openings)

  • Exterior glass: 12 × $6.00 = $72.00
  • Interior glass: 12 × $5.00 = $60.00
  • Screens: 12 × $3.00 = $36.00
  • Tracks and sills: 12 × $3.00 = $36.00
  • Quoted price: $204.00

Example production times for the per-window method (including 30 minutes of walk-around and setup): 12 × 5 minutes exterior + 12 × 4 minutes interior + 12 × 2 minutes per screen + 12 × 3 minutes per track + 30 = 198 minutes, or 3.3 worker-hours. On a two-person crew that is 1.65 hours on site. These times are the spreadsheet’s example times for a standard opening. They are likely too short if each opening really has eight panes, which is part of the point of comparing the two counts.

Priced per pane (96 panes)

  • Exterior glass: 96 × $1.50 = $144.00
  • Interior glass: 96 × $1.25 = $120.00
  • Screens: 12 × $3.00 = $36.00 (still one screen per opening)
  • Tracks and sills: 12 × $3.00 = $36.00 (still one track line per opening)
  • Quoted price: $336.00

Example production times for the per-pane method, plus the same 30 minutes of setup: 96 × 1.25 minutes exterior + 96 × 1.00 minute interior + 12 × 2 minutes per screen + 12 × 3 minutes per track + 30 = 306 minutes, or 5.1 worker-hours (2.55 hours on site for a two-person crew). Screens and tracks do not multiply by pane count, because you still handle 12 screens and 12 sets of tracks. The glass lines do.

The per-pane quote is $132.00 higher than the per-window quote on the same house ($336.00 − $204.00), and the example time is 1.8 worker-hours longer. That gap is a property of these example rates and this 8-pane count. It is not a rule that “per pane is always higher,” and it is not a market difference you should expect on every house. If the openings had been single-pane picture windows, applying $1.50 per pane would have underpriced the glass relative to the $6.00 per-window rate. Match the unit you count to the unit your rate was set for.

Check each quote against cost and a target margin

The spreadsheet’s example cost assumptions used here: labor at $24.00 per worker-hour (a stand-in for wage plus payroll costs, not a wage recommendation), a two-person crew, 15 minutes of paid drive time for each worker, supplies as in the default material lines, $0.70 per mile, $5.00 of equipment wear, and $18.00 of overhead per job ($600 a month of overhead spread over 400 jobs a year). The target margin in the file is 35% of the quoted price. The minimum price for that margin is cost ÷ (1 − 0.35). None of those figures is a typical cost or a recommended target.

Per-window quote of $204.00

  • Paid labor hours = 3.3 + (15 ÷ 60 × 2) = 3.8 hours; labor = 3.8 × $24.00 = $91.20
  • Materials $3.60, travel 12 × $0.70 = $8.40, equipment $5.00, overhead $18.00
  • Total cost = $126.20. Profit = 204.00 − 126.20 = $77.80. Margin = 38.14%
  • Minimum price for a 35% margin = 126.20 ÷ 0.65 = $194.15. Status: meets the example target, if you believe the 3.3-hour time. If the glass actually takes closer to the pane-method 5.1 hours, this cost is too low.

Per-pane quote of $336.00

  • Paid labor hours = 5.1 + 0.5 = 5.6 hours; labor = 5.6 × $24.00 = $134.40
  • Materials $6.96, travel $8.40, equipment $5.00, overhead $18.00
  • Total cost = $172.76. Profit = 336.00 − 172.76 = $163.24. Margin = 48.58%
  • Minimum price for a 35% margin = 172.76 ÷ 0.65 = $265.78. Status: meets the example target on these inputs.

The cheaper quote can still show a passing margin if the time you assigned to it is the standard-window time. Count the glass the way you timed it, then check the margin. These results are not typical margins and not a reason to choose one method for every job.

Questions

Should I always price divided-light windows per pane?

Not automatically. Per pane is usually the fairer unit when one opening holds many small pieces of glass, provided the pane rate was set for that size of glass. Some cleaners still quote per opening with a “divided-light” or “French” surcharge. Either way, count the work you will actually do. This page does not prescribe a method.

Why is the per-pane quote only $132 more, not eight times the per-window quote?

The pane rates in the example ($1.50 and $1.25) are much lower than the per-window rates ($6.00 and $5.00), and screens and tracks stay priced per opening. Eight panes at $1.50 exterior is $12.00, not 8 × $6.00. The $132.00 gap is the arithmetic on this house and these rates, not a rule of thumb for every grid window.

Are these typical window-cleaning prices?

This page does not say so. $204.00 and $336.00 are the totals from the spreadsheet’s example default rates on a 12-opening, 96-pane sample house. They are not a market average and not a recommended bid. For published homeowner ranges and a different worked house, see the bidding guide.

Is this pricing advice?

No. It is a planning layout with a worked example. It is not a market rate, not a forecast, and not tax, legal, insurance or business advice. Ladder and upper-story work is dangerous; follow your own safety rules. Verify every number before you send a quote.
