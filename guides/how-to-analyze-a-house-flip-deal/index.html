How to analyze a house flip deal, with a worked example

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

A house flip is a purchase you plan to renovate and sell, not hold as a rental. Before you make an offer, it helps to write down four groups of numbers: what you will pay to buy the property, what you will spend to repair it, what it will cost to hold it until it sells, and what you think it can sell for after the work. From those you can estimate profit, the cash you have to put in, and a well-known rule of thumb called the 70% rule.

This page works one illustration all the way through. The numbers are example data, chosen so the arithmetic is visible. They are not market rates, not a typical result, and not a recommendation to buy or to offer any particular price. Replace every figure with your own. Planning aid only; not financial, tax, legal or investment advice.

After-repair value (ARV)

After-repair value, or ARV, is the sale price you are planning for once the work is done, in the condition you actually intend to deliver. It is an input, not a result. If the ARV is too high, every later number looks better than it should. This guide does not tell you how to pick an ARV from comps or an appraisal; type a conservative figure you can defend, and test a lower one too.

In the example, ARV is $285,000.

Rehab and holding costs

Rehab is the work to get the property from the condition you buy it in to the condition that supports that ARV. A useful budget is split by category (kitchen, bathrooms, flooring, paint, exterior, electrical and plumbing, HVAC, permits, a contingency, and other) so a missed trade does not hide in one lump sum. The example uses a total rehab of $46,200, built from kitchen $12,000, bathrooms $8,000, flooring $6,000, paint and interior $3,500, roof and exterior $7,000, electrical and plumbing $4,000, permits $1,500, and a $4,200 contingency. Those lines are sample amounts, not quotes or typical job costs.

Holding costs are what you pay while you own it and it is not yet sold: property tax, insurance, utilities, HOA fees, lawn care, and similar items. In the example the hold is 5 months. Annual tax is $2,400 and insurance is $1,200, so those two contribute $2,400 × 5 / 12 = $1,000 and $1,200 × 5 / 12 = $500. Utilities at $200 per month and lawn care at $80 per month add $280 × 5 = $1,400. Holding costs excluding loan interest are $2,900.

Loan interest is separate. This example uses a short-term, interest-only style loan: 20% down on a $185,000 purchase, so the loan is $148,000, at 11% per year, plus 2 points paid at closing. Points are $148,000 × 0.02 = $2,960. Interest for five months is $148,000 × 0.11 × 5 / 12 = $6,783.33. That is interest-only; it is not a fully amortizing mortgage payment. Principal is treated as repaid from the sale. A 0% rate or a 100% down (all-cash) purchase makes the interest and points $0. Financing terms vary; use the rate and points you were actually quoted.

The 70% rule

A common rule of thumb for a flip offer is:

Maximum offer = ARV × 70% − total rehab

In the example, $285,000 × 0.70 = $199,500, minus $46,200 rehab = a maximum offer of $153,300. The example purchase price is $185,000, which is $31,700 above that figure, so this illustration does not meet the 70% rule.

The rule is only a starting filter. It does not subtract holding costs, loan interest, points, purchase closing costs or selling costs, and it does not say that a price at or under the result is a good deal. A property can fail the rule and still show a profit once you run the full cost, or pass the rule and still lose money. Treat “Yes” or “No” as a flag, not a verdict.

A worked example, start to finish

Purchase price $185,000 and purchase closing costs $5,550. Rehab $46,200. Hold 5 months; holding costs $2,900; interest-only interest $6,783.33; points $2,960. ARV $285,000. Selling costs in this illustration are a 6% agent commission plus 1% seller closing costs, which is 7% of $285,000 = $19,950, plus $1,500 of other selling costs, for a total of $21,450.

  • Net sale proceeds = 285,000 − 21,450 = $263,550.00
  • Total cost before sale = 185,000 + 5,550 + 2,960 + 46,200 + 2,900 + 6,783.33 = $249,393.33
  • Profit = 263,550.00 − 249,393.33 = $14,156.67
  • Cash invested = down payment $37,000 + 5,550 + 2,960 + 46,200 + 2,900 + 6,783.33 = $101,393.33
  • ROI = 14,156.67 ÷ 101,393.33 = 13.96%
  • Annualized ROI = (1 + 0.1396)12 / 5 − 1 = 36.84%. That compounds this deal’s ROI as if you could repeat a five-month hold over a year. It is a planning figure, not a forecast of what you would earn in a year, and a short hold makes the annualized number look larger.

So this illustration shows a profit of $14,156.67 and still fails the 70% rule, because the rule ignores several of the costs that were subtracted from profit. That is the point of running both views. Change any input and both results move. These figures are not typical and not a promise of what a flip earns.

Questions

Does a profit in this example mean the 70% rule is wrong?

No. The rule and the profit figure answer different questions. The rule is a quick cap on the offer from ARV and rehab only. Profit subtracts closing costs, points, holding costs, interest and selling costs as well. In the example, the purchase is above the 70% maximum offer and the profit is still $14,156.67. Neither result is a recommendation.

What if I pay all cash?

Use a 100% down payment. The loan, points and interest are then $0. Cash invested is the full purchase price plus closing costs, rehab and holding costs. ROI is still profit divided by that cash invested; it will usually look smaller than a financed deal that earned a similar profit with less cash in, because more of your money is tied up.

Are these typical flip returns?

This page does not say so. 13.96% ROI and 36.84% annualized ROI are the arithmetic on the sample numbers above. They are not a market average, not a target, and not a forecast.

Is this financial advice?

No. It is a planning layout with a worked example. It is not a market rate, not a valuation, and not tax, legal or investment advice. Verify every number and consult qualified professionals before buying, renovating or selling a property.
