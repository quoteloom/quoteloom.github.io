How to price lawn care and seasonal mowing contracts

Published 2026-11-22 · Quoteloom

A weekly mowing price copied from a neighbor can look fine until you add the drive, the unload time, the fuel, and the four fertilizer visits you also promised. A seasonal or monthly contract makes that worse if the first number was too low: the customer now expects a year of underpriced stops, not one cheap visit.

This guide shows how to price each service from the lot size and a per-stop minimum, multiply by the visits you will actually make, apply a contract discount only if you mean it, and then check labor, equipment time, travel, materials and disposal against a target margin. The math matches the free Quoteloom lawn care calculator. Example rates below are the calculator’s starting points, not a recommended price list. Published 2026 ranges are cited and linked; they do not all agree, and that is the point of reading more than one source.

What homeowners are reading in 2026

Angi’s lawn-mowing cost guide (updated March 17, 2026) puts the national average at $123 per visit, with a typical range of $49 to $203. It also lists $50 to $200 per acre, or $0.01 to $0.06 per square foot, and a size table that prices a quarter-acre lawn at $15 to $50 and a one-acre lawn at $50 to $200. Labor is listed at $25 to $60 per hour, or roughly $30 to $80 per mowing visit. Frequency plans on the same page run $65 to $100 per week, $200 to $400 per month, or $2,000 to $4,000 per year.

Angi’s broader lawn-care cost guide (updated September 24, 2026) is not only about mowing. It puts the national average for lawn care at $300, with most homeowners paying $100 to $500, and mowing at $50 to $205 per visit, again $0.01 to $0.06 per square foot or $50 to $200 per acre. Other 2026 service bands include aeration at $75 to $200, fertilizing at $71 to $380, and leaf removal at $300 to $600. Angi says the average residential lot is between a quarter and a fifth of an acre (8,712 to 10,890 square feet) and lists monthly lawn-care totals by size, including $200 to $400 for a quarter acre. Labor is listed at $35 to $190 per hour, with most homeowners paying $35 to $70.

HomeAdvisor’s lawn care and mowing page (updated June 17, 2026) is a useful second source because some of its bands match Angi and some do not. It also cites an average around $123, typically $49 to $203, and the same per-acre and per-square-foot mowing bands. Routine labor is $35 to $70 per hour, with more complex work up to $190. Packaged visits are listed at $50 to $200 weekly and $100 to $300 monthly. Aeration, fertilization and leaf-removal ranges match Angi.

Those figures cannot all be a single rate card. A quarter-acre mow at $15 to $50 sits well below a $123 national average visit. A per-acre band of $50 to $200 implies $12.50 to $50 on a quarter acre, while $0.01 to $0.06 per square foot on 10,890 square feet is $109 to $653. Angi’s monthly mowing plan ($200 to $400) lines up with its monthly lawn-care band for a quarter acre, but HomeAdvisor’s monthly visit range is lower ($100 to $300). That is not a problem you have to “fix.” Published averages mix cities, yard sizes and job types, and your quote still has to come from your labor, your route and your time on site.

Price each visit from the lot, then apply a minimum

Convert the lot to square feet if you think in acres. One acre is 43,560 square feet, so a quarter acre is 10,890 square feet. Price each service as the greater of (square feet ÷ 1,000 × your price per 1,000 square feet) and your minimum for that stop, then multiply by the number of visits:

Visit price = the greater of (lot sq ft / 1,000 × price per 1,000 sq ft) and the per-visit minimum.

Season price for that service = visit price × visits.

A minimum matters because a small yard can still take a full setup, a drive, and a dump of clippings. Without one, a 3,000-square-foot lawn priced only by the foot will not cover the stop. Production rates (square feet the crew actually covers per hour) belong in the cost stack, not in the price formula: they tell you how long the visit takes, which drives labor and equipment time.

Example starting points from the calculator, again not market rates: mowing at $6 per 1,000 square feet with a $40 minimum; edging and string trimming at $1.50 per 1,000 square feet with a $10 minimum; leaf cleanup at $14 per 1,000 square feet with a $120 minimum and a $25 disposal fee; fertilizing at $5 per 1,000 square feet with a $45 minimum and $2.20 of materials per 1,000 square feet; core aeration at $9 per 1,000 square feet with an $80 minimum. That mowing example is $0.006 per square foot, which is below the published $0.01 to $0.06 band. Do not treat it as a survey of what crews charge.

Turn the visits into a contract, then check the floor

Add every service’s season price, then apply a contract discount only if the package really saves you selling time or fuel. Divide by the months you will bill to get a monthly payment. An eight-month contract (for example April through November) and a twelve-month payment plan are the same season of work with a different billing choice.

  • Season contract = (sum of season prices) × (1 − discount).
  • Monthly payment = contract / months billed.
  • Hours = sum of (visits × lot sq ft / production rate) + trips × setup minutes / 60, where trips = the highest visit count among the services (the days you actually roll the truck).
  • Labor = hours × crew size × wage per worker-hour. Equipment = hours × equipment cost per hour. Travel = trips × miles × cost per mile. Materials and disposal fees are added by service and visit.
  • Season cost = labor + equipment + travel + materials + disposal.
  • Minimum contract for your target margin = season cost / (1 − target margin). If the quoted contract is below that floor, the discount is too steep or the visit prices are too low.

The free calculator follows those formulas and does not allocate monthly overhead (insurance, shop rent, office time) to the contract. Those costs still have to come out of the margin. Worked example: a quarter-acre, eight-month contract

Use the calculator’s default job, which is clearly labeled as example data. The lot is 0.25 acres (10,890 square feet). The season includes 28 mows, 14 edging visits, 28 string-trimming visits, 2 leaf cleanups, 4 fertilizer visits and 1 core aeration, with a 5% contract discount billed over 8 months. The crew is two people at $20 per worker-hour, equipment is $9 per hour, setup is 10 minutes per trip, and the round trip is 8 miles at $0.75 per mile. Target margin is 30%.

Per-visit prices at the example rates, each already above its minimum: mowing $65.34, edging $16.34, trimming $16.34, leaf cleanup $152.46, fertilizing $54.45, aeration $98.01. Season prices are $1,829.52, $228.69, $457.38, $304.92, $217.80 and $98.01, which add up to a $3,136.32 season price before the discount. After 5%, the contract is $2,979.50, or $372.44 per month over eight months.

Production time is 34.67 crew-hours. Trips follow the highest visit count, which is 28, so setup adds 28 × 10 / 60 = 4.67 hours, for 39.33 hours in total. Labor = 39.33 × 2 × $20 = $1,573.33. Equipment = 39.33 × $9 = $354.00. Travel = 28 × 8 × $0.75 = $168.00. Fertilizer materials = 4 × 10.89 × $2.20 = $95.83. Leaf disposal = 2 × $25 = $50.00.

Season cost = $1,573.33 + $354.00 + $168.00 + $95.83 + $50.00 = $2,241.16.

Profit = $2,979.50 − $2,241.16 = $738.35. Margin = $738.35 / $2,979.50 = 24.8%, which is below the 30% target. The minimum contract for that target is $2,241.16 / 0.70 = $3,201.65, or $400.21 per month. The example contract does not clear the floor.

That is the useful result, not a failure of the math. A 5% “loyalty” discount on rates that were only a starting point is enough to miss the target. The published monthly band Angi lists for a quarter-acre lawn ($200 to $400) would also miss this floor at the low end, and the $372.44 example payment sits inside that band while still being too low for a 30% margin on these costs. Published ranges are not a substitute for the cost stack.

If the contract is below the floor

You can raise visit prices, drop or shrink the discount, or cut visits you cannot staff profitably. Billing the same season over fewer months does not fix the total. Dropping the 5% discount in this example would put the contract back at $3,136.32, which is still $65.33 short of the $3,201.65 floor. The visit prices were already thin once labor, equipment and travel were counted.

A cheaper weekly mow that omits edging, trimming, fertilizer and cleanup is a different job and needs its own cost check. Do not assume a “mow-only” price can subsidize later fertilizer or leaf visits. Also remember what is still missing: monthly overhead, rained-out days you already collected for, and sales tax if you charge it. Those lines make the floor higher, not lower.
