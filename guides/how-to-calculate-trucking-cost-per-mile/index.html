Owner-operator trucking: how to calculate cost per mile

Published 2026-11-22 · Quoteloom

Not financial, tax, legal or business advice. A load can look like a decent rate per mile and still lose money once empty miles, fuel and the truck payment are in the picture. This guide walks through a cost-per-mile model you can compute by hand or in the free Quoteloom trucking cost-per-mile calculator. The worked example uses the calculator’s example inputs. They are not market rates, not a typical cost per mile, and not a bid you should copy.

This page cites only figures from sources we opened: ATRI’s Analysis of the Operational Costs of Trucking: 2026 Update (2025 fleet data, released July 2026), the EIA’s weekly on-highway diesel prices, and the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook entry for heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers. They are not your costs or a freight rate you will be offered tomorrow.

The cost-per-mile identity

Separate costs that stay about the same no matter how many miles you run (fixed) from costs that rise with every mile, loaded or empty (variable). Divide the fixed costs by the miles you actually expect to run, including deadhead:

  • Annual fixed costs = 12 × (truck payment + ELD or compliance software + other monthly fixed costs) + insurance + permits and licenses.
  • Fuel cost per mile = fuel price per gallon / miles per gallon (or 0 if MPG is 0).
  • Variable cost per mile = fuel + maintenance and repairs + tires + average tolls per mile.
  • Fixed cost per mile = annual fixed costs / annual miles (including empty miles).
  • Total cost per mile = fixed cost per mile + variable cost per mile + any per-mile driver pay.
  • Break-even rate per total mile = total cost per mile.
  • Break-even rate per loaded mile = total cost per mile / the share of annual miles that are loaded. If that share is 0, break-even on paid miles is not reachable.
  • For one load: trip miles = loaded miles + deadhead miles. Revenue = loaded miles × the per-mile rate. Load cost = total cost per mile × trip miles. Profit = revenue − load cost. The break-even loaded rate for that trip = load cost / loaded miles.

That split matters because you are usually paid only on loaded miles, but fuel, tires, maintenance and a share of the truck payment still accrue on the empty ones. A 500-mile loaded haul with 80 miles of deadhead is a 580-mile trip for the truck.

Fuel is the line that moves the fastest

Fuel cost per mile is the pump price divided by MPG, so it changes whenever either input changes. On November 16, 2026, the EIA’s U.S. average on-highway diesel price was $5.937 a gallon (including taxes), down 19 cents from the prior week but $2.069 higher than a year earlier. Regional averages on the same survey ranged from $5.547 on the Gulf Coast to $6.948 on the West Coast and $7.733 in California. The EIA updates the survey weekly, so use the current figure — or, better, what you actually pay on your lanes.

Miles per gallon is yours to measure. ATRI’s 2026 report found that participating fleets’ truck-tractors averaged 7.43 MPG in 2025, barely up from 7.41 in 2024, with a wide range by operating weight: 7.56 MPG at 60,001–70,000 pounds, 7.10 MPG at 70,001–80,000 pounds, and 6.20 MPG above 80,000 pounds. Shorter or heavier work will not match a highway fleet average. Start with your own MPG; do not bid as if you will always run 7.43.

What the 2025 industry averages actually are — and are not

ATRI’s July 15, 2026 release reported that the industry-average cost to operate a for-hire combination truck in 2025 was a record $2.336 per mile, 3.4 percent above $2.260 in 2024. Excluding fuel, costs rose 4.2 percent to $1.854 a mile. Table 8 in the report listed these 2025 averages:

Year over year, tolls rose 13.2 percent, repair and maintenance 8.6 percent, driver benefits 6.6 percent and tires 6.4 percent. Fuel was almost flat in the 2025 annual average (up 0.2 percent), which is not a 2026 pump price — ATRI noted fuel costs were up 5.9 percent in the first quarter of 2026, and the EIA price above is far higher than $0.482 a mile at 7.43 MPG would imply. Totals also differed by sector: $2.21 a mile for truckload, $2.39 for specialized and $2.58 for less-than-truckload. Participating truck-tractors averaged 85,991 miles in 2025.

Those figures are still not “your” number. The report covers for-hire combination-truck operations. ATRI says the 2026 study represents 182,248 combination trucks (5.2 percent of those registered) and 14.67 billion miles (7.6 percent of combination-truck miles). Fleets with 251 to 1,000 trucks made up the largest share of respondents, and ATRI, citing ATA’s American Trucking Trends 2025, notes that 91.5 percent of fleets operate with 10 or fewer trucks. A single truck or a different region will not land on $2.336. Use the table as a checklist of cost centers, then fill in your books.

Deadhead is why the loaded rate has to be higher

ATRI calls non-revenue mileage “the single-greatest drain on costs and operations” in many cases. For non-tank fleets in the 2025 data, empty mileage was 16.5 percent (16.7 percent in 2024, and still elevated versus recent pre-recession years). Tank carriers, which often cannot take a backhaul, ran empty on 44.3 percent of their miles. If 16.5 percent of your miles are empty, only 83.5 percent are paid, and a $1.80 cost per all miles already implies a break-even loaded rate of about $2.16 before any profit or factoring fee. The free calculator’s example uses an 84 percent loaded-mile share; replace it with your own empty-mile percentage.

Driver pay: a cost, a target wage, or the leftover

How you treat pay changes the result. BLS reports that the median annual wage for heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers was $58,640 in May 2025, or $28.19 an hour, with the lowest 10 percent under $40,140 and the highest 10 percent above $79,380. The same handbook notes that these drivers are usually paid by the mile, plus bonuses, and that some long-distance drivers, especially owner-operators, are paid a share of shipping revenue.

ATRI’s 2025 fleet average was $0.818 a mile in driver wages and $0.210 in benefits, or $1.028 combined — the first year that combined figure topped a dollar a mile in that series. A carrier that hires a driver has to put a number like that in the cost column. A solo owner-operator can leave driver pay at $0 and treat the calculator’s profit as what is left, or enter a target wage (per mile or, in the spreadsheet, an annual salary) so a paycheck is counted as a cost. Neither official source is a wage you must pay or will earn. ATRI also reported that the average contracted owner-operator rate paid by carriers slipped to $2.08 per mile in 2025, while truckload marginal costs were $2.21 and the all-sector average was $2.336. That $2.08 is what participating carriers said they paid contracted owner-operators, not a spot quote and not a recommended rate.

A worked example (calculator defaults, not a recommended bid)

The free calculator ships with this clearly labeled example so you can see the math and then overwrite every input. It is not a survey of owner-operator costs and it is not tied to the ATRI averages above.

Example inputValue
Annual miles (including deadhead)100,000
Loaded-mile share84%
Truck payment$2,200 per month
Insurance$12,000 per year
Permits and licenses$2,000 per year
ELD / compliance software$40 per month
Other fixed costs$250 per month
Fuel price / MPG$5.90 per gallon / 7.0 MPG
Maintenance / tires / average tolls$0.20 / $0.05 / $0.04 per mile
Driver pay$0 per mile (solo; profit is what is left)
Example load$2.40 per loaded mile, 500 loaded miles, 80 deadhead miles

Annual fixed costs = 12 × ($2,200 + $40 + $250) + $12,000 + $2,000 = $43,880. Fixed cost per mile = $43,880 / 100,000 = $0.4388. Fuel cost per mile = $5.90 / 7.0 = $0.8429. Variable cost per mile = $0.8429 + $0.20 + $0.05 + $0.04 = $1.1329. Total cost per mile = $0.4388 + $1.1329 + $0 = $1.5717. The break-even rate per loaded mile = $1.5717 / 0.84 = $1.8710.

The example load is 580 trip miles. Revenue = 500 × $2.40 = $1,200. Load cost = $1.5717 × 580 = $911.56. Load profit = $288.44. The break-even loaded rate for this trip = $911.56 / 500 = $1.8231 — a little lower than the annual $1.8710 figure because this trip’s empty-mile share (80 / 580) is below the 16 percent annual assumption. Change any input and the results move with it.

What to do with the number

Cost per mile is a floor for planning, not a freight-rate quote. A load still has to clear that floor after deadhead and after any factoring or broker fee you actually pay (those percentage fees are in the spreadsheet, not on the free page). The model also leaves out income taxes, depreciation, fuel-tax credits and unpaid detention. If you collect a fuel surcharge, fold it into the rate you type and use a fuel price that matches the same period.
