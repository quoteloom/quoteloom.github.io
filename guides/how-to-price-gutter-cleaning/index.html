How to price gutter cleaning per linear foot

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Gutter cleaning looks like a quick job priced off a quick number. That is how it gets underbid: the whole house is quoted at the one-story rate, the packed back corner under the oak is treated like the clean front run, the downspouts are “thrown in,” and a short ranch job costs nearly as much to set up and drive to as a big one.

This guide builds a gutter cleaning price from the work: measuring linear feet by roof level, a per-foot rate for each height, condition and downspout lines, a minimum, travel, and a worked example with a cost check. Example rates show the math; they are not a price list for your area.

What 2026 cost guides say homeowners pay

Angi’s 2026 gutter cleaning cost guide (updated October 17, 2026) gives a national average of $168, based on 37,376 verified projects from Angi pros, with most homeowners paying $119–$234. It says pros charge $0.95–$2.25 per linear foot and that most homes have 125 to 200 linear feet of gutters. By height it lists:

Angi also notes that some contractors price by home square footage instead (around $0.40 per sq ft for the first floor and $0.80 for the second), that a steeper roof slope adds 15%, that neglected gutters cost 10% to 50% more to clean, and that downspout cleaning is a common add-on at $50 to $100. It says that when gutters sit on different levels, a contractor charges by the length on each level, so only the higher runs carry the higher rate.

HomeAdvisor’s gutter cleaning cost guide (updated June 20, 2026) shows the same $119–$234 range, $168 average and $0.95–$2.25 per linear foot. It says contractors may not clean downspouts and extenders unless asked, and that the job averages about one hour for an average single-story home and two to three hours for taller houses.

These are national figures, not a quote for a specific house. Use them to spot a rate that is far off, not as your rate card.

Step 1: measure linear feet by roof level

Linear feet means the running length of gutter along the roof edge. Measure it, do not guess it from the house’s square footage:

  • Walk the drip line with a measuring wheel, or measure each eave on a map or plan and confirm on site.
  • Write each run down by level: first-story eaves, second-story eaves, porch or garage roofs, and any third-story or steep sections.
  • Count downspouts and note any that drain into underground pipes.
  • Note what slows you down: gutter guards that must come off, roof pitch, landscaping or decks under the ladder, power lines near the roof edge, and how full the gutters look.

Put the measured footage on the quote. If the customer disputes the price, the conversation is about numbers you can both check.

Step 2: set a per-foot rate for each height

Height changes your speed, your equipment and your risk, so price each level separately, the way Angi describes contractors doing it. Build each rate from your own production:

rate per linear foot = (labor cost per hour ÷ feet cleaned per hour + supplies per foot) ÷ (1 − target margin)

Time a few real jobs from parking to driving away, separately for one-story and two-story work, before you trust a production number. Ladder moves, bagging debris and flushing are part of the time.

Per foot vs. flat tiers

Flat prices (“single-story up to 150 ft,” “two-story up to 200 ft”) are easy to sell. Keep them, but make each tier per-foot math rounded to a clean number, with a stated footage limit. Anything outside the tier goes back to per-foot pricing.

Step 3: price condition, downspouts and guards as lines

  • Condition: a gutter with a season of leaves is not a gutter with years of packed debris and plants. Set a written surcharge for heavy buildup (Angi reports 10% to 50% more for neglected gutters) and apply it to the runs that need it. Take a photo before you start.
  • Downspouts: HomeAdvisor notes that downspouts and extenders may not be cleaned unless the customer asks. Decide what “gutter cleaning” includes, and price flushing or clearing clogged downspouts as a line (per downspout or per job). If a downspout drains into a buried pipe you cannot clear, say so on the quote.
  • Gutter guards: removing and reinstalling guards or screens takes time. Price it per foot or per section, and say whether you clean the guards themselves.
  • Steep roofs: if pitch forces you to work only from the ladder, add a slope adjustment (Angi cites 15%).
  • Debris haul-away: bagging and removing debris, or leaving it in the customer’s yard waste, should be stated. Haul-away is time and possibly a disposal cost.
  • Repairs: loose hangers, leaking seams and sagging sections are separate work. Note them, photograph them and quote them separately instead of fixing for free.

Step 4: safety time is job time

Gutter cleaning is ladder work, and safe setup takes time you should price. OSHA’s portable ladder safety quick card says to look for overhead power lines before handling a ladder, keep three points of contact while climbing, place the ladder base a quarter of the working length from the wall, and extend a ladder at least 3 feet above the point of support when accessing an elevated surface. Moving and re-setting the ladder correctly along every run is part of your production rate, not something to rush to hit a low price.

Step 5: set a minimum and charge travel

A short single-story job still needs the drive, the ladder setup, cleanup and the paperwork. A minimum charge covers that fixed time. Work it out from your fixed cost per visit: minimum = (setup and cleanup time × labor cost + travel + supplies and wear) ÷ (1 − target margin), then round up. Show it on your rate card so it is applied the same way every time.

For travel, use your real cost per mile on the round trip. Keep the minimum for nearby jobs and add a travel line beyond a set radius.

Worked example: a two-story house with a packed back run

A house has 120 ft of first-story gutters and 90 ft of second-story gutters (210 ft total), six downspouts, and heavy buildup along the back under the trees. One technician, ladder work. These are example rates and costs to show the method, not rates for your market.

LineExample mathPrice
First-story gutters120 ft × $1.10$132.00
Second-story gutters90 ft × $1.50$135.00
Heavy debris surcharge25% × $267.00$66.75
Downspout flush6 × $10$60.00
Quoted price$393.75, rounded up$395.00

The example per-foot rates sit inside Angi’s one-story and two-story bands. The total is above Angi’s $180–$360 two-story project range because the quote includes heavy debris and six downspout flushes as separate lines; that is the point of itemizing.

Now check the price against cost. Example production: 80 ft per hour on the first story and 50 ft per hour on the second, so 120 ÷ 80 + 90 ÷ 50 = 3.30 hours of cleaning. Heavy debris adds 25% time: 3.30 × 1.25 = 4.125 hours. Add 5 minutes per downspout (0.50 hours) and 0.50 hours to set up, bag debris and clean up: 5.125 worker-hours.

Labor cost should be what a worker-hour really costs you, not just the wage. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 reports wages and salaries were 70.0% of private-industry compensation costs. Using that ratio as a rough guide, an example $20 hourly wage costs about $20 ÷ 0.70 = $28.57 per hour.

  • Labor = 5.125 × $28.57 = $146.43
  • Bags, gloves, flush water and consumables (example) = $10.00
  • Ladder, scoop and blower wear allowance (example per job) = $10.00
  • Travel = 24 round-trip miles × $0.70 (example) = $16.80

Job cost = $183.23. Profit = $395.00 − $183.23 = $211.77, a 53.6% margin before overhead. The minimum price for a 40% target margin = $183.23 ÷ 0.60 = $305.38. Insurance, the vehicle, marketing and rain days still come out of that profit.

The underbid version: all 210 ft at the one-story rate with no condition or downspout lines is 210 × $1.10 = $231.00. The work and the cost are the same $183.23, so profit drops to $47.77, a 20.7% margin, below the 40% target.

Now a small job: a one-story ranch with 100 ft of gutters and four downspouts. At $1.10 per foot that is $110. The time is 100 ÷ 80 + 4 × 5 minutes + 0.50 setup = 2.08 worker-hours ($59.52), plus $10 consumables, $10 wear and $16.80 travel: a cost of $96.32 and a 12.4% margin. The floor at a 40% margin is $96.32 ÷ 0.60 = $160.54, so an example $165 minimum (41.6% margin) protects the small job.

Common gutter pricing mistakes

  1. One rate for the whole house. Price each roof level at its own rate.
  2. Guessing the footage. Measure, and write the linear feet on the quote.
  3. Free heavy-debris work. Packed gutters take longer; use a written condition surcharge.
  4. Free downspouts. Say whether flushing is included, and price clogged downspouts as a line.
  5. Fixing things for free. Hangers, seams and sagging sections are a separate quote.
  6. No minimum. Short jobs carry nearly the same setup and drive time as long ones.
  7. Using wage instead of full labor cost. Payroll costs and ladder moves belong in the cost.
  8. Rushing ladder setup to hit a low price. Safe setup is part of the job time.
