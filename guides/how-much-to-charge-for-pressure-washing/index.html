How much to charge for pressure washing (2026 pricing guide)

Published 2026-11-21 · Quoteloom

A homeowner who googles “how much to charge for pressure washing” usually lands on a national average and tries to work backwards from it. That number is useful as a sanity check. It is a weak way to set your price. Two 2,000-square-foot houses are not the same job: one is a single-story vinyl wash you can reach from the ground; the other is a two-story house plus a stained driveway, 180 feet of gutters, and a 25-mile round trip. If you quote both at the same “average house” price, one of them is probably a loser.

This guide walks through how to build a quote from the surface, a minimum charge, and the cost of doing the work — the same method as the free Quoteloom pressure washing calculator. The rates in the worked example are starting points from that calculator, not a recommended price list for your city. Where a published 2026 range is cited, the source is linked.

What 2026 cost guides actually say

Homeowners will often mention a number they saw online before you arrive. It helps to know what those pages currently show, and what they are measuring.

Angi’s 2026 pressure-washing guide (updated October 13, 2026) puts the national average cost to pressure wash a house at $312, based on 51,092 verified projects reported by Angi Pros. Most of those projects fall between $213 and $449. Angi also lists a wide per-square-foot band of $0.10 to $0.50, with flat, easy surfaces such as driveways and patios toward the low end. For driveways specifically, Angi reports $100 to $250 on average, or $0.20 to $0.40 per square foot, with dirtier or larger drives running higher.

HomeAdvisor’s pressure-washing cost page (updated June 17, 2026) publishes the same typical range of $213 to $449 and the same $312 average, plus a few figures Angi does not lead with: about $50 to $100 per hour per crew member, a minimum charge of about $100 to cover equipment and travel, $0.20 to $0.40 per square foot for most outdoor concrete, $0.50 to $1.50 per linear foot for gutter work (often $50 to $150 on a single-story house), and $0.50 to $1 per square foot or about $450 to $700 for a roof. HomeAdvisor also lists one-story houses at $150 to $750, two-story houses at $400 to $1,400, and three-story houses at $700 to $1,800.

Treat those figures as what customers may expect, not as your price list. The “average job” mixes small driveway washes with whole-house work. A per-square-foot rate that works on a large, open driveway will underpay you on a small, awkward one, which is why a minimum charge exists.

Price the surface, then apply a minimum

A practical quote starts with each service on its own line:

Service price = the greater of (size × your price per unit) and your minimum for that service. Skip any service whose size is zero. Add the service prices to get a subtotal. If you offer a discount, apply it to the subtotal, not to one line in isolation.

The minimum is doing real work. A 200-square-foot sidewalk at $0.18 per square foot is $36, which does not pay for hookup, travel, or the time it takes to unroll hose. HomeAdvisor’s “about $100” minimum is one published reference point; many operators set a higher floor once they know their true cost to roll out. The example rates below, which also ship in the free calculator, use service-level minimums ($250 for a house wash, $150 for a driveway, $125 for gutters) so a tiny add-on cannot sneak through at a toy price. They are examples so you can see the math, not a recommendation.

Worked example: house, driveway, and gutters

Take a 2,400-square-foot house wash, a 900-square-foot driveway, and 180 linear feet of gutters. No deck or roof. You offer a 5% discount. The round trip is 24 miles at $0.70 per mile. Setup is 0.75 hours, the crew is two people, and each worker-hour costs you $22 (wage plus the payroll costs you actually pay, not just the hourly rate you hand someone). Your target margin is 35%. These are the default inputs on the free calculator.

Service prices:

  • House wash: 2,400 × $0.20 = $480, which is above the $250 minimum, so $480.
  • Driveway: 900 × $0.18 = $162, above the $150 minimum, so $162.
  • Gutters: 180 × $1.50 = $270, above the $125 minimum, so $270.

Subtotal = $480 + $162 + $270 = $912. After a 5% discount, the quoted price is 912 × 0.95 = $866.40.

Hours on the work itself: 2,400 / 1,500 + 900 / 800 + 180 / 100 = 1.60 + 1.125 + 1.80 = 4.525 hours. Add 0.75 hours of setup and you have 5.275 crew hours.

Then the cost lines:

  • Labor = 5.275 × $22 × 2 = $232.10.
  • Chemicals (example costs of $0.02, $0.01, and $0.03 per unit) = 2,400 × 0.02 + 900 × 0.01 + 180 × 0.03 = $62.40.
  • Travel = 24 × $0.70 = $16.80.

Job cost = $232.10 + $62.40 + $16.80 = $311.30.

Profit = $866.40 − $311.30 = $555.10.

Margin = $555.10 / $866.40 = 64.1%, which clears a 35% target.

The price you would need in order to hit the target exactly is:

Minimum price for the target = job cost / (1 − target margin) = $311.30 / (1 − 0.35) = $478.92.

In this example the quoted $866.40 is well above that floor, so the 5% discount is affordable. If a customer talked you down to $450, you would be quoting below the cost-plus floor for a 35% margin. That is the point of computing the minimum price before you send the number, not after the customer has already heard it.

When a “standard minimum” is not enough

Suppose, instead, you only wash the 2,400-square-foot house and you quote a flat $250 because that is your house-wash minimum. Hours fall to 2,400 / 1,500 + 0.75 setup = 2.35 crew hours. Labor is 2.35 × $22 × 2 = $103.40. Chemicals are $48.00. Travel is still $16.80. Job cost is $168.20. Profit at a $250 quote is $81.80, and the margin is 32.7% — just under a 35% target. The minimum price for 35% would be $168.20 / 0.65 = $258.77.

Nothing is “wrong” with a $250 minimum in the abstract. On this trip, with this crew cost and this mileage, it is $8.77 short of the target. That is a small gap, and it is exactly the kind of gap a calculator is for. Raise the quote a little, raise the minimum, shorten the drive, or accept a thinner margin on purpose — but do it with the numbers in front of you.

What this page is not counting

The free calculator, and the worked examples above, include labor, chemicals, and travel. They do not allocate equipment wear or monthly overhead (insurance, marketing, office time, a truck payment) to the job. Those costs are real. If you ignore them, a 64% margin on paper is fatter than the margin you will keep. The Quoteloom pressure washing spreadsheet adds equipment wear and allocated monthly overhead to each job, and it keeps a job log and a seasonal projection so you can see whether a busy month actually covers the bills.

Labor cost should be what a worker-hour truly costs you, including payroll taxes and any benefits you pay, not only the wage. Time production rates on real houses: a soft wash on oxidized siding is not the same hour as a light maintenance wash.

How to use this on the next quote

  1. Measure (or get a reliable size for) each surface. Leave anything you will not wash at zero.
  2. Apply your price per unit, then your minimum, for each service. Add them up. Apply a discount only if you still clear the minimum price for your target margin.
  3. Estimate crew hours from your production rates, plus setup. Multiply by crew size and your true labor cost per worker-hour.
  4. Add chemicals and mileage. That is the job cost on this simplified model.
  5. Compute profit, margin, and the minimum price for your target. If the quote is below that floor, raise it or walk away.
