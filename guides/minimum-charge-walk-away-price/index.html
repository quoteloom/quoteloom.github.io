How to set a minimum charge and a walk-away price

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Every service visit has a cost before any work starts: the drive there and back, unloading and packing up, the quote and the invoice, the vehicle, and a share of your monthly bills. Small jobs carry almost all of that cost and very little work to pay for it. A minimum charge makes sure every visit covers it. A walk-away price is the number below which you politely decline a job instead of working for nothing.

This guide works out both from your own numbers, adds a profit-per-hour check, and covers when a lower “while we’re here” price makes sense. It applies to house cleaning, lawn care, pressure washing, window and gutter cleaning, and similar visit-based work. Example rates show the math; they are not a price list for your area.

Why minimums are normal

Customers see minimum charges in published cost guides. Angi’s 2026 driveway pressure washing cost guide (updated October 16, 2026), for example, notes that because square-footage pricing is the norm, smaller projects like a one-car driveway may trigger a minimum charge to cover the company’s travel and overhead costs. Stating your minimum up front, on your rate card and quote, is clearer than quietly padding small jobs.

Step 1: add up your fixed cost per visit

List what one visit costs you no matter how small the job is:

  • Paid drive time: round trip, for every person you pay, at the full cost of a labor hour.
  • Setup and cleanup: unloading, protecting the area, packing up, a final walk-around.
  • Admin: quoting, scheduling, messages, invoicing and payment follow-up for that customer.
  • Vehicle: round-trip miles × your real cost per mile (fuel, maintenance, insurance, depreciation). Track your own; the IRS standard mileage rate for business use is 76 cents per mile for July 1 – December 31, 2026, which is a tax deduction rate, not your actual cost, but it is a useful reference point.
  • Consumables and wear that every visit uses.
  • Overhead share: monthly fixed costs (insurance, software, phone, marketing, storage) ÷ jobs per month.

Labor should be the full cost of an hour, not just the wage. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 reports wages and salaries were 70.0% of private-industry compensation costs. As a rough guide, full labor cost ≈ wage ÷ 0.70. If you work alone, use the hourly amount you need to pay yourself, not zero.

Step 2: turn it into a minimum charge

minimum charge = (fixed cost per visit + cost of the smallest job you would do) ÷ (1 − target margin), rounded up to a clean number.

Use the same target margin you use for normal quotes, so a minimum job earns like any other job. Then write it down: “Minimum service charge $X within Y miles.” Beyond your normal radius, either add a travel line or raise the minimum by the extra drive cost.

Step 3: set a walk-away price for every quote

Your quoted price aims at your target margin. Your walk-away price is the lowest you will accept when a customer pushes back:

walk-away price = full job cost ÷ (1 − lowest margin you accept)

The full job cost includes everything in Step 1 plus the work time and materials for this job. Decide the lowest acceptable margin before the conversation, not during it. If the customer’s number is below the walk-away price, change the scope (fewer rooms, fewer add-ons, a smaller area) rather than the price, or decline.

Check profit per hour, too

Margin alone can hide a bad job. Also check profit per hour = (price − full job cost) ÷ total hours including drive and setup. A long job at a thin margin can tie up a day you could have filled with better work. If your calendar is full, your walk-away price should be higher; if it is empty, a lower margin may still beat an unpaid day, as long as the price stays above cost.

Worked example: a solo operator

  • Walk-away price at a 20% lowest acceptable margin: $200.77 ÷ 0.80 = $250.96, so $251.

At the customer’s $220 the job technically makes money, but $4.27 an hour above the operator’s own pay is not worth a 4.5-hour block. The operator offers a smaller scope at a price above $251, or declines.

A “while we’re here” add-on: if a neighbor asks for a 30-minute job while the operator is already on the street, there is no extra drive, setup is shared and admin is folded into the main visit. The cost is about 0.5 × $28.57 + $5 of materials = $19.29, and $19.29 ÷ 0.60 = $32.14 hits the 40% target. A lower add-on price can be fine here because the fixed cost is already paid by the main job. As a stand-alone visit, the same job is a $170 minimum.

How to say no without losing the customer

  • State the minimum before you quote: “Our minimum visit is $170 within 15 miles.”
  • Offer scope instead of discount: “For $250 I can do the front and sides; the back can be added next visit.”
  • Offer timing instead of discount: bundling with a visit you already have on their street.
  • Decline politely and leave the door open: “That’s below what I can do it for, but I’d be glad to help if the scope changes.”

Do not invent deadlines or fake scarcity to push a customer over your price. A clear rate card and an itemized quote do that job honestly.

Common mistakes

  1. No minimum at all. Small jobs then lose money on the drive alone.
  2. Counting your own time as free. Solo operators still need an hourly cost.
  3. Forgetting admin and overhead. Quoting, invoicing and insurance are real costs per visit.
  4. Deciding the walk-away price during the haggle. Set it before you talk price.
  5. Discounting price instead of scope. Cut the work, not the margin.
  6. Never revisiting the numbers. Recalculate when fuel, wages, overhead or job volume change.
