How to set a handyman hourly rate, and when to switch to a flat fee

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Hourly and flat fees are two ways to state the same job. Hourly follows the clock when you cannot see the full scope yet. A flat fee is a single number for a scope you have already defined. This page shows how the two methods diverge when a job runs short or long, then ties the number to a written estimate. It is a planning aid, not a recommended rate, not a license to do regulated work, and not pricing, tax or legal advice.

The worked job, rates and travel figure are the same illustrations as the handyman estimate guide: a fictional deck-and-gate repair at $60.00 an hour, $35.00 an hour of labor cost, and travel at the IRS business mileage rate of 76 cents per mile for July–December 2026, used only as a travel-cost illustration, not as tax advice. They are not typical handyman rates and not a market survey.

What homeowners are quoted in 2026, as published

Angi’s handyman cost guide, updated 23 September 2026 and based on 103,764 verified projects from Angi Pros, states a national average project cost of $407, with most homeowners paying between $178 and $687. On labor, it says handyman rates typically range from $50 to $150 per hour, “though flat-fee pricing is common for well-defined tasks,” and that most handymen charge a minimum call-out fee that covers the first hour of labor. It also says materials purchased on the customer’s behalf often carry a 10% to 30% markup. Those are Angi figures for what homeowners pay, not a recommended billing rate and not your market.

When hourly is the clearer method

  • The scope is not visible until you open something (a leak, a noise, a door that “just won’t close”).
  • The customer wants you to “see what’s going on” before agreeing to a full repair.
  • You have not done this exact job often enough to trust a time estimate.

Write a rate, a minimum (often the first hour, which is what Angi describes as common), and a cap or a check-in if the clock may run long. The estimate still needs a scope, exclusions and a validity date, as in the estimate guide.

When a flat fee is the clearer method

  • You have a written scope and you have timed similar jobs before (a latch reset, a set of boards, a fan swap with access confirmed).
  • The customer wants one number they can accept, not an open clock.
  • You are bundling several small tasks into one visit, so a single total is easier to read than many small hourly lines.

Build the flat fee from estimated hours × your hourly rate, plus materials and travel, then keep that total even if you finish early. If you find more work, stop and agree a change. A flat fee does not automatically cover a new scope. For very small stops, a minimum still matters: at the example $60.00 rate, 45 minutes of labor is only $45.00 before materials and travel, while a two-hour labor minimum is $120.00. The minimum-charge guide covers that floor in more detail.

Worked example: the same $310.20 job, hourly vs flat

Fictional scope, repeated from the estimate guide: replace two loose deck boards and reset a sagging gate latch. Exclusions: no staining, no work on the rest of the fence, no haul-away if the customer keeps the old boards. Estimated labor is 4.0 hours at $60.00 = $240.00. Materials: 8 ft of treated board $22.00 + exterior screws $8.00 + a gate latch $25.00 = $55.00. Travel: 20 miles × $0.76 = $15.20. Combined estimate: $310.20.

If you send that $310.20 as a flat fee for the written scope, the customer owes $310.20 whether the work takes 3 hours or 5.5, unless the scope changes.

  • If the job takes 3.0 hours and you had billed hourly: labor 3.0 × $60.00 = $180.00, plus $55.00 and $15.20 = $250.20. The flat fee is $60.00 higher than that hourly total. You keep the time you saved, which is the usual point of a flat fee on work you know well.
  • If the job takes 5.5 hours and you had billed hourly: labor 5.5 × $60.00 = $330.00, plus $55.00 and $15.20 = $400.20. The flat fee is $90.00 lower. You absorbed the overrun.

A cost check for you, not for the customer’s PDF, using the estimate guide’s example labor cost of $35.00 an hour (not a wage recommendation):

  • At the estimated 4.0 hours: labor cost $140.00 + $55.00 + $15.20 = $210.20. Profit at the $310.20 flat fee = $100.00.
  • At 3.0 hours: labor cost $105.00 + $55.00 + $15.20 = $175.20. Profit at the flat fee = $135.00.
  • At 5.5 hours: labor cost $192.50 + $55.00 + $15.20 = $262.70. Profit at the flat fee = $47.50, a 15.3% margin on the $310.20 fee. That check still leaves out equipment wear and monthly overhead, so the paper margin is fatter than what you keep if you ignore those.

Use hourly when you cannot defend the 4.0-hour estimate. Use a flat fee when you can, and when you can live with the 5.5-hour case. Neither path is prescribed.

Questions

Is $60 an hour a typical handyman rate?

This page does not say so. $60.00 is the illustration already used in the estimate guide so the arithmetic is visible. Angi, updated 23 September 2026, published a homeowner range of $50 to $150 per hour. That range is not a recommended bid and not a guarantee of what your market will pay.

Should every small job have a minimum?

A minimum exists so a 20-minute stop still covers the drive and setup. Angi says most handymen charge a minimum that covers the first hour. This page does not tell you to copy that rule. The two-hour, $120.00 labor minimum above is only an illustration at the $60.00 example rate.

Is a flat fee the same as a contract?

No. A flat fee is a way to state the price for a written scope. A contract, a license, a permit and insurance are separate questions and are not covered here.

Is this pricing advice?

No. The $60.00 rate, $35.00 labor cost and $310.20 total are illustrations. They are not typical rates and not a guarantee of acceptance or profit. If the work requires a licensed contractor, a permit, or insurance you do not have, do not bid it as if you did.
