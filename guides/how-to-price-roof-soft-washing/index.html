How to price roof soft washing (and when not to use high pressure)

Published 2026-11-24 · Quoteloom

Roof cleaning pays well and is easy to underbid. A roof that looks like a quick “spray and rinse” can be steep, two stories up, surrounded by landscaping and covered in growth on the shaded side. Price it like a driveway and you pay for the extra time, chemicals and risk yourself.

This guide builds a roof soft wash price from the work: measuring, a per-square-foot rate, adjustments for pitch, stories and material, a minimum, chemical and safety costs, and a worked example. Example rates show the math; they are not a price list for your area.

First, the method: why roofs get a soft wash

A soft wash means applying a cleaning solution at low pressure, letting it work, and rinsing gently. On asphalt shingles, that is not just a preference. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association (ARMA) technical bulletin on algae discoloration (revised June 2024) says not to use a power washer or any type of brush or broom to clean algae from the roof, and that high-pressure washing systems are likely to damage asphalt roofing. It also warns that scrubbing may loosen and remove granules.

ARMA says household bleach (sodium hypochlorite) and water may lighten algae, but only temporarily; mixing directions vary by shingle maker; and other chemicals should not be used without the maker’s approval. It calls for a 15–20 minute dwell, a gentle rinse, and warns the treated roof is slippery. Owens Corning’s algae guidance calls for covering vegetation first, a 1:1 bleach-and-water mix left about 10–20 minutes, no scrubbing or high-pressure washing, and thoroughly flushing the roof and plants. Its May 2026 maintenance article adds that a pressure washer can displace granules and potentially void warranties. Read the instructions for the specific shingle on the job.

For pricing, this means: know the roof material before you quote, name the method (“low-pressure soft wash”) in the quote, and do not match a cheaper pressure-washing bid on a shingle roof. That is a different, riskier service.

What 2026 cost guides say homeowners pay

Angi’s 2026 roof cleaning cost guide (updated October 26, 2026) puts professional roof cleaning at $296–$626, averaging $461. For a 1,600 sq ft roof it lists a soft wash on asphalt, clay or concrete at $560–$900, a chemical wash on tar-and-gravel or rubber at $450–$840, a low-pressure wash on wood or slate at $600–$1,100 and a high-pressure wash on metal at $350–$700. By size, ranges run from $150–$450 for 750 sq ft to $600–$1,800 for 3,000 sq ft on easily accessible roofs, with a premium for steep or complex roofs. Angi says labor can be up to 90% of the price and a cleaning takes two to five hours on average.

HomeAdvisor’s roof cleaning page (updated June 19, 2026) shows the same range and average, says most pros quote per square foot or per “square” (100 sq ft), some charge $35–$80 per hour, and moss or algae prevention treatments add $100–$250.

These are national figures across several methods, not a quote for a specific roof. Use them to spot a rate that is far off.

Step 1: measure the roof along the slope

You spray and rinse the roof surface, so price the area along the slope, not the house’s floor area. If you measure the footprint (the roof outline seen from above, including overhangs), multiply it by a pitch factor. Pitch is written as rise per 12 inches of run; the factor is the square root of 1 + (rise ÷ 12)²:

A 1,800 sq ft footprint at 8/12 is about 2,163 sq ft of roof. If your measurement is already along the slope, do not apply the factor again.

Step 2: choose a pricing method

Per square foot (or per square)

The standard method. Set a base rate for an easy roof (one-story, walkable asphalt, normal growth) from your own production: rate per sq ft = (labor cost per crew-hour ÷ sq ft per crew-hour + chemical cost per sq ft) ÷ (1 − target margin). Time several real roofs from setup to cleanup before you trust any production figure.

By roof type, pitch and stories

Keep a small rate table: a base rate per material, plus a per-square-foot surcharge for steep pitch (anchors, harnesses, slower footing) and for each extra story. Angi says three-story homes cost significantly more because of access and risk. Material changes method and speed:

  • Asphalt shingle: low-pressure soft wash with the shingle maker’s mix and dwell time.
  • Clay and concrete tile: soft wash; HomeAdvisor says clay cannot take pressure washing. Tile can crack underfoot, so work is slower.
  • Wood shake and slate: low-pressure only; Angi’s band for these is the highest in its table.
  • Metal: both guides say metal can take a controlled higher-pressure wash; check the coating maker’s instructions.
  • Flat tar-and-gravel or rubber: chemical wash with a suitable cleaner.

Hourly

Useful as an internal check. Customers prefer a firm price, so estimate hours and quote a total.

Step 3: set a minimum charge

Every roof job has fixed costs: driving, setting ladders and anchors, covering plants, mixing, rinsing landscaping and packing up. Set a minimum from your own cost to show up and apply it as job price = the greater of (sum of line items) and your minimum.

Step 4: cost out chemicals and plant protection

Do not guess chemical cost. Track it:

  1. Cost per gallon of finished mix = (bleach or sodium hypochlorite + surfactant) cost ÷ total gallons, at the shingle maker’s ratio.
  2. Record gallons of mix used per roof and divide square feet cleaned by it. After a few jobs you have your own coverage by material and growth level.
  3. Chemical cost per sq ft = cost per gallon of mix ÷ sq ft per gallon.

Plant protection is part of the manufacturer method: cover vegetation below the roof and rinse it thoroughly afterward. That takes sheeting, water and paid time on every job. Price it as a line or build it into the minimum, and add time for heavy landscaping or a pool under the eaves.

Step 5: price the safety work

Roof cleaning combines height with a surface you are making slippery. Owens Corning recommends fall protection and slip-resistant footwear on the roof. If you have employees, OSHA’s general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.28, generally requires protecting each employee on a surface with an unprotected side or edge 4 feet or more above a lower level with guardrails, safety nets or personal fall protection systems. On residential roofs, an employer that can show those are not feasible or create a greater hazard must use a fall protection plan and training instead; the rule presumes they are feasible. Low-slope roofs have separate rules. Confirm what applies to you with OSHA or a safety professional; this is not safety or legal advice.

For pricing: setting anchors, tying off, moving ladders and working slower on a wet, steep roof are paid time, and harnesses, ropes and anchors wear out. Charge for them per job.

That sits toward the upper end of Angi’s size bands ($400–$1,200 for 2,000 sq ft; $500–$1,500 for 2,500 sq ft), which fits a steep two-story roof.

Now check the price against cost. Example production: 700 sq ft of roof per crew-hour on this steep roof, 0.75 crew-hours to set up ladders, anchors and plant covers, and 0.5 crew-hours for the final rinse and pack-up. That is 2,163 ÷ 700 + 0.75 + 0.5 = 4.34 crew-hours, inside Angi’s two-to-five-hour range. With a two-person crew, that is 8.68 worker-hours.

Labor cost should be what a worker-hour really costs you, not just the wage. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 reports wages and salaries were 70.0% of private-industry compensation costs. Using that national average as a rough guide, an example $20 wage becomes $20 ÷ 0.70 = $28.57 per worker-hour. Yours may differ.

  • Labor = 8.68 × $28.57 = $248.00
  • Chemicals (example $0.04 per sq ft, from your own tracking) = 2,163 × $0.04 = $86.52
  • Plant covers and consumables (example) = $15.00
  • Fall protection wear and replacement (example per-job allowance) = $20.00
  • Travel = 30 round-trip miles × $0.70 = $21.00

Job cost = $390.52. Profit = $1,131.50 − $390.52 = $740.98, a 65.5% margin before overhead. The minimum price for a 35% target margin = $390.52 ÷ 0.65 = $600.80. Insurance, equipment, a truck and marketing still come out of that profit.

Now a small roof: a one-story house with a 900 sq ft footprint at 4/12 pitch is 949 sq ft of roof. At $0.40 that is $379.60, below the example $400 minimum, so the quote is $400. Time: 949 ÷ 700 + 0.75 + 0.5 = 2.61 crew-hours, or 5.22 worker-hours ($149.14). Add $37.96 of chemicals, $15 of covers, $20 for fall protection and $21 of travel, and the cost is $243.10. Margin at $400 is 39.2%. Setup and rinse were nearly half the time, which is why the minimum exists.

Common underbidding mistakes

  1. Pricing the footprint, not the slope. On the example roof, that alone leaves out 363 sq ft of work.
  2. One rate for every roof. Steep two-story tile is not walkable one-story shingle.
  3. Free plant protection. Covering and rinsing plants is paid time.
  4. Guessing chemical cost. Track gallons used per roof and price per gallon of finished mix.
  5. Skipping safety costs. Anchors, tie-off time and gear wear are job costs.
  6. No minimum. Small roofs carry nearly the same setup and rinse time as large ones.
  7. Matching pressure-washing bids on shingle roofs. Explain the method shingle makers recommend instead.
  8. Using wage instead of full labor cost. Payroll costs and drive time belong in the cost.
  9. Promising permanent results. ARMA says the effect is temporary. Say so, and offer a maintenance visit.
