How to price a move-out or deep cleaning without underbidding

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

A one-time deep clean or move-out clean is not a bigger standard clean. The home has months of buildup, the customer wants inside the oven, fridge and cabinets, the landlord inspection is the deadline, and you will probably never get the recurring visits that make a low first price worth it. A flat “move-out special” quoted over the phone is the classic way to lose money on it.

This guide builds the price from the work: a walk-through or photos, time from square footage and condition, add-ons priced as lines, paid drive time, a minimum, and a worked example with a cost check. Example rates show the math; they are not a price list for your area. For recurring visits, see how to price house cleaning jobs.

What 2026 cost guides say homeowners pay

Angi’s 2026 move-out cleaning cost guide (updated October 14, 2026) gives a national average of $360, with most homeowners spending $120–$420. It says labor averages around $50 per hour, that rates usually range from $25 to $50 per cleaner, and that companies typically send two cleaners. Its base cleaning cost by size:

For apartments it lists $90–$130 for a studio, $100–$190 for 1 bed / 1 bath, $140–$210 for 2 bed / 2 bath and $180–$300 for 3 bed / 2 bath. It notes that interior appliance and cabinet cleaning is not typically included in a basic move-out package and usually adds $10 to $40 per service, and lists other add-ons such as carpet cleaning ($120–$240), window cleaning ($80–$300), tile and grout cleaning ($100–$200) and garage cleaning ($70–$200). It also says a home with significant dirt, grime or staining needs a deep cleaning, which costs more.

Angi’s 2026 deep cleaning cost guide (updated September 29, 2026) gives a national average of $260, with most homeowners spending $180–$375, and pros typically charging $0.10 to $0.30 per square foot, $25 to $70 per hour, or $25 to $60 per room. It lists move-in/move-out cleaning of an empty home, including inside cabinets, closets and appliances, at $140–$500, and deep cleaning by size from $100–$200 (up to 800 sq ft) to $330–$900 (2,500+ sq ft).

These are national figures, not a quote for a specific home. The base ranges assume a base clean; your add-ons and the home’s condition come on top. Use them to spot a rate that is far off, not as your rate card.

Step 1: see the home before you commit to a number

Condition is the biggest unknown in a one-time clean, and it is the part a phone quote misses. Ask for a walk-through, a video call, or photos of the kitchen (including inside the oven and fridge), every bathroom, and the floors. Record:

  • Square footage, bedrooms and bathrooms.
  • Empty or furnished, and whether the utilities (water, power) will be on.
  • Condition by room: light dust, normal use, or heavy buildup (grease, soap scum, hard-water stains, pet hair).
  • What the customer expects inside: oven, fridge, cabinets, closets, windows, blinds, baseboards, walls.
  • The deadline (key handover or inspection) and any landlord checklist they have to meet.

Put the scope in writing. “Move-out clean” means different things to different people; a checklist on the quote prevents a free re-clean later.

Step 2: estimate the time, then price it

Square footage is a good starting point for time, but condition changes it a lot. Build your estimate in cleaner-hours:

cleaner-hours = sq ft ÷ your sq ft per cleaner-hour for this service × condition factor + add-on time

Use a separate production rate for deep and move-out work, timed on real jobs, not your standard-clean rate. A condition factor (for example 1.00 for normal use, higher for heavy buildup) set from the photos keeps you from re-arguing every quote. Then check the price against the cost: minimum price = job cost ÷ (1 − target margin).

Per square foot, per room or hourly?

Angi reports all three in use. Per square foot or per room is easy for customers to compare; hourly protects you on unknown condition but makes the final bill uncertain for the customer. A practical middle path: quote a firm price built from your time estimate, list the add-ons separately, and state what happens if the home is in clearly worse condition on arrival than in the photos.

Step 3: price the add-ons as separate lines

Angi notes interior appliance and cabinet cleaning is not typically in a basic move-out package. Put each extra on its own line with its own time, so the customer can choose and you are paid for every hour:

  • Inside oven, inside fridge, inside cabinets and drawers, inside closets.
  • Interior windows and tracks, blinds, baseboards, wall spot-cleaning.
  • Garage, patio or balcony.
  • Carpet shampoo, tile and grout: if you do not do these, say so and leave them off.

Step 4: pay the drive, set a minimum

Drive time between jobs is paid time if you pay your cleaners for it, and with two cleaners it counts twice. Use the full cost of a labor hour, not just the wage. Set a minimum for one-time deep and move-out work that covers the drive, setup and the hours a small but dirty apartment still takes. Write it on your rate card so you apply it the same way every time.

Worked example: an empty 1,800 sq ft house

A 3-bed, 2-bath, 1,800 sq ft house, empty, normal wear except heavy grease in the kitchen and buildup in one bathroom. The customer wants inside the oven, fridge and kitchen and bath cabinets, plus 14 interior windows. Two cleaners. These are example rates and costs to show the method, not rates for your market.

Move-out clean, base1,800 sq ft × $0.22$396.00

Quoted price$630.40

Now the time. Example production for an empty-home move-out: 300 sq ft per cleaner-hour, so 1,800 ÷ 300 = 6.00 cleaner-hours, × 1.15 for condition = 6.90. Add-ons: oven 40 min, fridge 30 min, cabinets 60 min, windows 14 × 6 min = 84 min, a total of 214 min = 3.57 cleaner-hours. Cleaning time is 10.47 cleaner-hours, about 5.2 hours on site for two cleaners. The round-trip drive is 0.75 hours for each cleaner: 1.50 paid hours. Paid labor: 11.97 hours.

Labor cost should be what a worker-hour really costs you, not just the wage. The BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for June 2026 reports wages and salaries were 70.0% of private-industry compensation costs. Using that ratio as a rough guide, an example $18 hourly wage costs about $18 ÷ 0.70 = $25.71 per hour.

  • Labor = 11.97 × $25.71 = $307.71 (unrounded hours and rate)
  • Supplies, degreaser, pads and consumables (example) = $25.00
  • Travel = 20 round-trip miles × $0.70 (example) = $14.00

Job cost = $346.71. Profit = $630.40 − $346.71 = $283.69, a 45.0% margin before overhead. The minimum price for a 35% target margin = $346.71 ÷ 0.65 = $533.41. Insurance, equipment, marketing and unbooked days still come out of that profit.

Underbid 1, the phone special: a flat $250 “move-out clean” sits inside Angi’s $180–$300 base band for 1,500–2,000 sq ft. For this house it loses $96.71 on cost alone (a −38.7% margin), because the price covers a base clean and the job is a base clean plus buildup plus five add-ons.

Underbid 2, skipping the condition line: the same quote without the 15% buildup charge is $571.00. The time does not change, so the margin falls to 39.3%: still above target here, but the buffer is gone if the job runs long.

If your number is far above the published ranges, do not just cut it. Re-check your production times, offer a smaller base scope with the add-ons as options, or decide this is a job you do not take at the customer’s price. See how to set minimum charges and a walk-away price.

Common move-out and deep cleaning pricing mistakes

  1. Quoting by phone without photos. Condition decides the time; see it first.
  2. Using the standard-clean rate. Deep and move-out work needs its own production rate.
  3. Free appliances and cabinets. Angi notes they are not typically in a basic package. Price them.
  4. Vague scope. A written checklist prevents free re-cleans after the landlord walk-through.
  5. Forgetting the second cleaner’s drive time. Two cleaners means paid travel counts twice.
  6. Using wage instead of full labor cost. Payroll costs belong in the cost.
  7. Discounting a one-time job like a recurring one. There may be no second visit to make it back.
  8. No minimum. A small but dirty apartment can take as long as a clean house.
