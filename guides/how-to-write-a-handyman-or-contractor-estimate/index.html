How to write a handyman or contractor estimate, with a worked example

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

An estimate a customer can understand is mostly a document problem, not a slogan problem: what you will do, what it costs, what you will not do, and how long the number holds. This page walks through those parts, then works one fictional small job in full. It is a planning aid, not a promise the customer will accept the number, not a license to do regulated work, and not pricing, tax or legal advice.

The example rates and hours are labeled as illustrations. They are not typical handyman rates and not a market survey. If the work requires a licensed contractor, a permit, or insurance you do not have, say so and do not bid it as if you did.

What to put on the estimate

  • Who and where. Your name, a way to reach you, the customer’s name, and the job address.
  • Scope. The work in plain language, room by room or task by task, so a later change is obvious.
  • Line items. Labor (hours × your rate, or a flat labor line), materials (listed, not a lump if you can help it), and travel if you charge it.
  • Exclusions. What is not included (for example, painting, debris haul-away, or hidden rot you cannot see until you open something).
  • A validity date. How long the number holds, especially if material prices can move.
  • A deposit, only if that is actually your term. Write the amount due to book and the amount due when the work is finished.

Worked example: a small deck and gate repair

Fictional scope: replace two loose deck boards and reset a sagging gate latch at one house. Exclusions in this illustration: no staining, no work on the rest of the fence, no haul-away of old boards if the customer keeps them.

  • Labor: 4.0 hours × $60.00 = $240.00.
  • Materials: 8 ft of treated board $22.00 + a box of exterior screws $8.00 + a gate latch $25.00 = $55.00.
  • Travel: 20 miles × $0.76 = $15.20. The 76-cent figure is the IRS published business mileage rate for July–December 2026, used here only as a travel-cost illustration, not as tax advice.

Estimate total: 240.00 + 55.00 + 15.20 = $310.20.

If you ask for a 50% deposit to hold the date, and only if that is actually your term: 0.50 × 310.20 = $155.10 due to book, and $155.10 due when the work is finished.

A cost check, for you, not for the customer’s PDF: if the labor you pay (or pay yourself) on this job is 4.0 hours × $35.00 = $140.00, plus the same $55.00 of materials and $15.20 of travel, your direct cost is $210.20. Profit at the $310.20 estimate is 310.20 − 210.20 = $100.00. That check does not include equipment wear or monthly overhead, so the margin on paper is fatter than the margin you keep if you ignore those.

After the job, compare what you estimated with what you actually spent. The quoted-versus-actual guide walks that review on a pressure-washing example; the same three columns (quoted price, planned cost, actual cost) work here.

Questions

Is an estimate the same as a contract?

Not by itself. This page is about a clear price document. A contract, a license, a permit and insurance are separate questions and are not covered here.

Should I show my cost check to the customer?

You do not have to. The $210.20 cost check in the example is for you, so you know whether $310.20 is a number you can live with. The customer’s PDF needs the scope, the price lines, the exclusions and the validity date.

What if I find more work once I start?

Stop and agree a change before you do the extra work. Do not add a surprise line after the fact. The validity date on the original estimate does not automatically cover a new scope.

Is this pricing advice?

No. The $60.00 labor rate, $35.00 labor cost and $310.20 total are illustrations so the arithmetic is visible. They are not typical rates and not a guarantee of acceptance or profit.
