How to bid residential and commercial window cleaning

Published 2026-11-23 · Quoteloom

Window cleaning looks easy to price: count the windows and multiply. But a single-story ranch with clean, ground-level glass and a two-story house with neglected screens and sprinkler spots can have the same window count. Give both the same per-window price and one of them probably loses money.

This guide builds a bid from the work itself: pricing methods, add-ons, height and access, minimums, recurring routes, and worked examples. Example rates show the math; they are not a price list for your city. Published 2026 figures are linked and dated.

What 2026 cost guides say homeowners pay

Angi’s 2026 window cleaning cost guide (updated October 8, 2026) puts the national average at $221 per visit, based on 25,969 verified projects reported by Angi Pros. Most homeowners pay between $150 and $303. Angi lists $4–$8 per window for inside-only or outside-only cleaning, $8–$16 for both sides, $12–$20 for post-construction cleaning and $15–$40 for high-rise or specialty access work. Add-ons: screens $2–$5 each, tracks and sills $2–$5 per window, hard-water removal $10–$30 per window. Minimums of $75–$150 often apply to small jobs, and recurring contracts typically get 10%–20% discounts.

HomeAdvisor’s window cleaning cost page (updated June 19, 2026) shows the same $150–$303 range and $221 average, or about $10 per window for a standard two-pane window, with single panes from $4 to $15. It says some companies switch from per-window to hourly pricing for third- or fourth-story work, and lists $45–$75 per hour for that.

These are consumer-facing national figures, not a quote for a specific building. The two guides disagree on some add-ons, which is a good reminder to price from your own time.

Four ways to price window cleaning

Per window

The most common residential method. You count windows, set a rate for one side and a rate for the other, and add extras. Customers understand it, but “a window” is not a fixed amount of work, so add size tiers. Angi’s size table runs from $4–$8 for small standard windows to $10–$20 for large picture, bay or bow windows.

Per pane

Better for divided-light, French and grid windows, where one opening can hold 6, 12 or more small panes. A per-window price badly underpays this glass. Define a pane in the quote (each separate piece of glass, each side).

Per hour

Useful for unusual jobs: a first visit to a neglected house, heavy post-construction cleanup, or tall work where access time is hard to predict. Customers cannot see the total in advance, so many cleaners estimate hours, quote a firm price, and use hourly only as an internal check.

Per square foot of glass (commercial)

For storefronts and commercial routes, glass area is often a fairer unit than window count. Measure width × height for each panel, add it up, and multiply by your rate. Set the rate from your own production: rate per sq ft = target revenue per worker-hour ÷ sq ft you clean per hour. Time yourself on real glass before you trust any number.

Inside vs outside, and the add-ons

Price inside and outside as separate lines. Interior work is slower than it looks (furniture, blinds, drop cloths, people). Separate lines also let you sell outside-only maintenance visits between full cleanings.

Treat these as add-ons with their own unit price, not as “included”:

  • Screens. Removing, washing and reinstalling takes real time. Price per screen.
  • Tracks and sills. Detail work. Price per window.
  • Hard water. Sprinkler or well-water spots need extra products and much more time, and some will not come off. Inspect first, price per window or pane, and state in writing that etched glass is not guaranteed.
  • Post-construction. Paint, stickers and adhesive need careful scraping. Quote it separately.

Height and access: ladders vs water-fed poles

Height changes the job more than window count does. Each second-story ladder window means placing, moving and climbing the ladder, and slopes, decks or landscaping slow it further. Add a per-window upper-story charge.

If you use ladders, follow the safety rules. The OSHA ladder standard for general industry, 29 CFR 1910.23, requires employers to make sure ladders are inspected before use each shift, that workers face the ladder and keep at least one hand on it when climbing, and that they do not carry loads that could throw them off balance. That time belongs in the bid.

A water-fed pole (a pole that feeds purified water to a brush, so the glass dries without spots) lets you clean many exterior windows from the ground. It removes many ladder moves, but the pole, the purification system, its consumables and set-up time at each stop are costs too. Time whichever method you will actually use on that house.

Work needing lifts or rope access is a different business; Angi’s $15–$40 per window band for high-rise or specialty access reflects that. Many small operators decline or subcontract it.

Minimum charges

Every stop has fixed costs: driving, parking, unloading, walking the job. “Just the four front windows” at $6 each is $24, which can cost more than that to deliver. Angi reports $75–$150 minimums on small jobs; set yours from your own cost to show up (drive and set-up time at your labor cost, plus mileage). Apply it as job price = the greater of (sum of line items) and your minimum.

Recurring route pricing

Regularly cleaned glass is faster, the stop is already on your route, and you skip selling a new customer. Angi reports recurring discounts of 10%–20%; check that the faster visit still clears your cost before offering one. Commercial storefronts usually get a fixed price per stop on a set schedule, priced by glass area or pane count, with a per-stop minimum. Group stops by area: a shop ten minutes from your next stop is worth more than the same shop across town.

Worked example: a two-story house, inside and out

Take a two-story house with 24 standard windows: 16 on the ground floor and 8 upstairs, reached by ladder. The customer wants inside and outside, screens, and tracks. Three windows near a sprinkler have hard-water spots. These are example rates, chosen inside the published bands above, to show the method:

Now check the bid against time. Example production times: 5 minutes per ground-floor exterior, 8 minutes per ladder window, 4 minutes per interior, 2 minutes per screen, 3 minutes per track, 15 minutes per hard-water window, and 30 minutes to walk the job and set up. That is 80 + 64 + 96 + 48 + 72 + 45 + 30 = 435 minutes, or 7.25 worker-hours (about 3.6 hours on site for a two-person crew).

Labor cost should be what a worker-hour really costs you, not just the wage. For reference, the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook reports a median hourly wage of $17.71 for janitors and building cleaners in May 2025 (the occupation BLS lists as washing windows and glass), with the lowest 10% earning under $13.77 and the highest 10% over $24.17. Payroll costs come on top; this example uses $24 per worker-hour.

  • Labor = 7.25 × $24 = $174.00
  • Supplies (example) = $15.00
  • Travel = 20 round-trip miles × $0.70 = $14.00

Job cost = $203.00. Profit = $477 − $203 = $274, a 57.4% margin before overhead. The minimum price for a 35% target margin = job cost ÷ (1 − 0.35) = $203 ÷ 0.65 = $312.31. A customer who pushes you to $300 is asking for less than that floor.

Now the recurring version. The customer signs up for quarterly outside-only visits. Exterior plus upper-story lines are $144 + $24 = $168. With a 15% recurring discount, the visit is $142.80. Time: 80 + 64 minutes of glass plus 20 minutes of set-up = 164 minutes (2.73 worker-hours), or $65.60 of labor. Add $5 of supplies and $4.20 of travel (6 miles, because the house now sits on a route). Job cost is $74.80, profit is $68.00, and margin is 47.6%. The discount works because the route cut travel; 40 miles out, it would not.

Worked example: a storefront route stop

A shop has 180 sq ft of ground-level glass, outside only, cleaned every two weeks. Suppose you clean flat ground-level glass at about 600 sq ft per worker-hour and you want $60 of revenue per worker-hour (both are example numbers; use your own). Your rate is $60 ÷ 600 = $0.10 per sq ft, so 180 × $0.10 = $18. But parking and set-up add about 5 minutes, so the stop takes about 0.38 hours, worth about $23 at your target. An example $25 per-stop minimum covers it; the glass-area price alone would not.

Common underbidding mistakes

  1. Counting windows, not panes. French and grid windows take far longer than their window count suggests.
  2. Bundling screens and tracks for free. On the example house, screens and tracks were 120 of the 435 minutes.
  3. Pricing upper floors like ground floors. Ladder moves and safety steps are paid time.
  4. Quoting hard water as a normal clean. Inspect it first, price it separately, and put limits in writing.
  5. No minimum. Small jobs far from your route can lose money even at a high per-window rate.
  6. Using wage instead of full labor cost. Payroll costs, paid drive time and set-up time all belong in the cost.
  7. Recurring discounts without a route. A discount only works if regular visits really are faster or closer together.
  8. Skipping overhead. Insurance, a vehicle, a water-fed-pole system, marketing and office time are real. The example margins above do not include them.
