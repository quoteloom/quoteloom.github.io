How to price house cleaning jobs

Published 2026-11-21 · Quoteloom

The fastest way to lose money on house cleaning is to copy a neighbor’s flat rate and hope the house is “about average.” Recurring clients will remember a low first-visit price. If that price did not cover paid drive time, supplies, and a margin you can live on, you have just trained a weekly or biweekly customer to expect a loss.

This guide shows how to price a visit from the method you already use — by the room, by the square foot, or by the hour — then subtract a frequency discount, add extras, and check the result against labor, supplies, and travel. The math matches the free Quoteloom house cleaning calculator. Example rates below are the calculator’s starting points, not a recommended price list. Published 2026 ranges are cited and linked; they do not all agree, and that is worth taking seriously.

What homeowners are reading in 2026

Angi’s professional house-cleaning guide (updated September 29, 2026) puts the national average at $176 per visit, based on 98,643 verified projects reported by Angi Pros. Most of those visits fall between $118 and $239. Angi prices a standard clean at $0.10 to $0.20 per square foot, a deep clean at up to $0.25 per square foot, and a move-out clean at up to $0.35 per square foot. In per-visit terms, Angi lists standard cleaning at $75 to $175, deep cleaning at $200 to $400, and move-out cleaning at up to $500.

HomeAdvisor’s house-cleaning price page (updated June 17, 2026) is a useful second source because it does not simply repeat the same per-square-foot band. It lists a national hourly range of $25 to $90 per individual (and $50 to $90 per hour in another hourly summary on the same page), weekly visits at $75 to $250 each, and a per-square-foot range of $0.05 to $0.10 for typical cleaning, with one-time jobs such as move-outs running as high as $0.20 per square foot.

Those two per-square-foot bands overlap only at the edges. Angi is generally higher on a standard clean ($0.10 to $0.20) than HomeAdvisor ($0.05 to $0.10). That is not a problem you have to “resolve.” It is a reminder that published averages mix cities, home sizes, and job types, and that your quote still has to come from your labor cost and your time on site. Four ways to set the cleaning price

Pick one method and stay consistent so you can compare jobs. The free calculator supports all three of the first options, then applies a minimum:

  • By square foot: price = home size × your rate per square foot for that service type.
  • By rooms: price = a base fee + (bedrooms × your per-bedroom rate) + (bathrooms × your per-bathroom rate). Bathrooms usually deserve their own line because they take more time than a spare bedroom.
  • By the hour: estimated cleaning hours × your hourly rate for that service. Estimated hours can come from size divided by a production rate (for example, square feet you actually clean per hour).
  • A flat visit price: fine once you have priced enough similar homes to know the flat rate still clears your minimum. A flat rate with no time estimate behind it is how underbidding starts.

Then: cleaning price = the greater of (the method result) and your minimum for that service type.

Example starting points from the calculator, again not market rates: a standard clean at $0.10 per square foot, or $60 + $20 per bedroom + $25 per bathroom, with a $120 minimum and a production rate of 500 square feet per cleaner-hour; a deep clean at $0.18 per square foot, or $100 + $30 per bedroom + $40 per bathroom, with a $220 minimum; a move-in or move-out at $0.22 per square foot, or $120 + $35 per bedroom + $45 per bathroom, with a $280 minimum.

Frequency discounts, if you offer them, apply to the cleaning price, not to add-ons. The calculator’s examples are 20% weekly, 15% biweekly, and 10% monthly, with 0% on a one-time visit. Add-ons (an oven, a fridge, interior windows, laundry) are added after the frequency discount. A final discretionary discount, if any, applies to the total.

In formula form:

Quoted price = (cleaning price × (1 − frequency discount) + add-ons) × (1 − extra discount).

Worked example: a 3-bed, 2-bath biweekly clean

Use the calculator’s default job: an 1,800-square-foot home, 3 bedrooms, 2 bathrooms, priced by the room, standard clean, biweekly, plus one inside fridge ($30). The crew is two cleaners. Each cleaner-hour costs you $18. You pay 20 minutes of drive time and 14 round-trip miles at $0.65 per mile. Target margin is 30%. No extra discount.

Cleaning price = $60 + 3 × $20 + 2 × $25 = $60 + $60 + $50 = $170, which is above the $120 standard-clean minimum, so $170 stands.

Biweekly discount of 15%: $170 × 0.85 = $144.50. Add the fridge: $144.50 + $30 = $174.50 quoted.

Time: 1,800 / 500 = 3.6 cleaner-hours for the house, plus 30 minutes for the fridge, is 4.1 cleaner-hours. With two people, time on site is 4.1 / 2 = 2.05 hours.

Paid time should include the drive. Paid hours = (2.05 + 20/60) × 2 = 2.3833 × 2 = 4.7667 hours. Labor = 4.7667 × $18 = $85.80.

Supplies = $8 for a standard clean + $1 for the fridge = $9.00. Travel = 14 × $0.65 = $9.10.

Job cost = $85.80 + $9.00 + $9.10 = $103.90.

Profit = $174.50 − $103.90 = $70.60. Margin = $70.60 / $174.50 = 40.5%, which meets a 30% target. The minimum price for that target is $103.90 / (1 − 0.30) = $148.43.

The $174.50 quote has room. That does not mean a deeper discount would still work.

Check the frequency discount against the floor

Keep every other input the same and switch the visit to weekly at the example 20% discount. Cleaning price is still $170. After 20% it is $136, plus the $30 fridge is a $166.00 quote. Labor, supplies, and travel do not automatically fall just because the visit is weekly — they might, over time, if a maintained house really is faster, but you should not assume that on day one. At the same $103.90 cost, profit is $62.10 and the margin is 37.4%. Still above 30%, and still above the $148.43 floor, but thinner than biweekly.

If you had also thrown in a courtesy $20 off, the quote would be under the floor. A frequency discount is only a good idea when the repeat work actually saves selling time or on-site time, and when the discounted price still clears job cost / (1 − target margin). If it does not, the discount is too steep, no matter what a competitor advertises.

A one-time deep clean is a different job

Same house, priced by the room as a deep clean, one-time (no frequency discount), with the oven ($35) and the fridge ($30) added. Deep-clean price = $100 + 3 × $30 + 2 × $40 = $270, above the $220 deep-clean minimum. Quoted price = $270 + $35 + $30 = $335.

Hours change because the production rate in the example is 300 square feet per hour for a deep clean, not 500: 1,800 / 300 = 6.0 cleaner-hours, plus 40 minutes for the oven and 30 minutes for the fridge, is 7.1667 cleaner-hours. On site with two people: 3.58 hours. Paid hours = (3.5833 + 0.3333) × 2 = 7.8333 hours. Labor = 7.8333 × $18 = $141.00. Supplies = $15 + $2 + $1 = $18.00. Travel is still $9.10. Job cost = $168.10. Profit = $166.90, margin 49.8%. The 30% floor is $168.10 / 0.70 = $240.14, so $335 clears it.

Notice that the deep clean is not “the standard price plus a bit.” It uses a slower production rate, a higher minimum, and more supply cost. If you bid a first-time or move-out job at your standard recurring rate, the hours will not fit.

Labor is paid time, including the drive

A quote that only multiplies “hours in the house” by a wage will look healthier than the job is. If you pay two cleaners for the ride across town, that time belongs in labor. Mileage is a separate line (fuel, wear, and the rate you actually use). The free calculator treats them that way: paid hours = (time on site + paid drive time) × crew size; travel = miles × cost per mile.

As with the pressure washing guide, this simplified model does not allocate equipment or monthly overhead to the visit. Insurance, scheduling time, and supplies you do not track per job still have to come out of the margin.
