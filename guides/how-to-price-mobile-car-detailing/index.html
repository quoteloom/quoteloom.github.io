How to price mobile car detailing

Published 2026-11-27 · Quoteloom

Mobile detailing gets underbid in two ways. The first is a flat “full detail” price that ignores the vehicle: a muddy three-row SUV with dog hair gets the same number as a clean sedan. The second is forgetting that the job starts when you leave, not when you uncoil the hose: the drive, the mileage and the water and power setup are real costs on every visit.

This guide builds a detailing price from the work: a package price for each vehicle size, a condition surcharge, add-ons, a travel fee, a minimum charge and a membership discount, then a worked example with a cost check. The rates in the example show the math; they are not a price list for your area.

What a 2026 cost guide says customers pay

Thumbtack’s mobile car detailing cost guide (last updated April 15, 2026) gives a national average range of $144–$254, with a low-end cost of $95 and a high-end cost of $390. It lists the factors behind the price as vehicle size, vehicle condition (dirt, pet hair, tough stains), the service package, location, and add-ons such as engine bay cleaning, headlight restoration and odor removal, which it says are typically priced separately from standard packages. It also notes that mobile detailers may need access to water and electricity.

Treat that range as a sanity check on what customers have seen, not as your price. It blends basic and full services across the country and says nothing about your time, travel or costs. The rest of this guide builds the price from those.

1. Price packages by vehicle size

Most detailers sell three or four named packages, for example an exterior wash, an interior detail and a full detail. Write down what each one includes, then time it on a car, an SUV, a truck and a van. Bigger vehicles take longer and use more product, so each size gets its own price and its own time. 2. Add a condition surcharge

A vehicle that hasn’t been cleaned in a year takes longer than one cleaned last month. Rather than guessing, set written condition levels (for example normal, dirty, very dirty, extreme) with a percentage that raises both the package price and the package time. Ask for photos before you quote, and say on the quote which condition you priced.

3. Price add-ons as separate lines

Pet hair removal, odor treatment, headlight restoration, engine bay cleaning, a spray sealant and a clay bar each take their own time and product. Price each one as a line with its own time and materials, so a customer who wants “everything” sees why the price went up, and a customer who wants the basics isn’t paying for extras.

4. Charge for travel and count the drive

Every mobile job has a drive there and back. Count the drive minutes as paid time and the round-trip miles at your real cost per mile. For reference, the IRS standard mileage rate for business use is 72.5 cents per mile for January 1 to June 30, 2026 and 76 cents per mile for July 1 to December 31, 2026. That is a tax deduction rate, not your actual cost, so work out your own figure from fuel, maintenance, insurance and vehicle payments. Many detailers include a free radius and charge a travel fee per mile beyond it, so far-away jobs still pay.

5. Set a minimum and a membership discount

A quick exterior wash still costs you the drive and the setup, so set a minimum charge for any visit. For repeat customers, a maintenance membership (weekly, biweekly or monthly) fills your calendar with planned work; a modest discount per visit can be worth it because the drive, the sales effort and the condition are easier to predict. Check the discounted price against your cost before you offer it.

Worked example: an SUV full detail on a monthly plan

A customer 14 miles away (22 minutes each way) wants a full detail on a dirty SUV, with pet hair removal, headlight restoration, a ceramic spray sealant and a clay bar, and signs up for a monthly plan. The package alone ($218.50) falls inside Thumbtack’s $144–$254 national range. The visit is above it because four add-ons and a travel fee are listed as separate lines; that is the point of itemizing.

Now check the price against cost. Example times: the full detail on an SUV takes 180 minutes, and the 15% surcharge makes it 207. The add-ons take 30 + 40 + 45 + 45 = 160 minutes, and setting up the tank and generator takes 15: 382 minutes, or 6.37 worker-hours on the vehicle. The drive is 22 × 2 = 44 minutes (0.73 hours), so the job uses 7.10 paid hours.

  • Labor = 7.10 × $22 per hour (example) = $156.20
  • Chemicals, sealant, towels, water and generator fuel (example) = $40.00
  • Vehicle = 28 round-trip miles × $0.70 (example) = $19.60

Job cost = $215.80. Profit = $409.65 − $215.80 = $193.85, a 47.3% margin before equipment wear and overhead, or $27.30 per paid hour. The minimum price for a 40% target margin = $215.80 ÷ 0.60 = $359.67. Allow an example $6 of equipment wear and $10 of overhead per job ($500 a month over 600 jobs a year) and the cost becomes $231.80, leaving $177.85 (43.4%). On a monthly plan this customer is worth 12 × $409.65 = $4,915.80 a year in revenue at these example rates.

The underbid version: the same work quoted as a flat $250 “full detail with extras,” with no travel fee. The cost is still $215.80, so profit drops to $34.20, a 13.7% margin, well below the 40% target.

Now a small job: an exterior wash on a car 6 miles away (12 minutes each way), using the customer’s water and power. At $50, the time is 60 minutes of washing + 10 minutes of setup + 24 minutes of driving = 1.57 paid hours ($34.47), plus $4 of chemicals and $8.40 of mileage: a cost of $46.87 and a 6.3% margin. The floor at a 40% margin is $46.87 ÷ 0.60 = $78.11, so an example $80 minimum (41.4% margin) protects the small job.

Common mobile detailing pricing mistakes

  1. One price for every vehicle. Price and time each package by size.
  2. Quoting without photos. Ask for photos and put the condition level on the quote.
  3. Free extras. Pet hair, odor and headlights take real time; list them as add-ons.
  4. Ignoring the drive. Drive time and mileage belong in the cost on every job.
  5. No minimum. Short jobs carry the same drive and setup as long ones.
  6. Discounting memberships blindly. Check the discounted price against your cost.
  7. Forgetting water and power. Your own tank and generator cost fuel, water and setup time.
  8. Using wage instead of full labor cost. Payroll costs belong in the hourly figure.
